   This theorem is of very diﬀerent nature from its discrete analogue [18]. Its
proof required an involved multi-scale analysis. Additive structure is analyzed at
each level (using a quantitative version of Freiman’s theorem due to Chang [13] in
Bourgain’s ﬁrst proof in [3]) and then multiplicative information is used to show
growth in size.
   When the discretized setting was introduced in [23], it was conceived as a general
strategy for proving results in the continuous regime (where sets are measured
by its Hausdorﬀ dimension) while using ideas and results in the discrete regime
where we have well-developed theory such as arithmetic combinatorics. Thus, one
of the original motivations of Bourgain’s theorem was the Erdős-Volkmann ring
conjecture [19] which asserts that no Borel measurable subring of R has Hausdorﬀ
dimension strictly between 0 and 1. This conjecture was also settled by Edgar and
Miller [17] using diﬀerent ideas. However, their proof is not quantitative. Moreover,
as anticipated in [23], Bourgain’s theorem also makes progress in geometric measure
theory, namely on the Falconer distance problem and the Furstenberg conjecture.
   It turns out that the inﬂuence of Bourgain’s theorem does not stop here. More
deep works have been done using this theorem. For example, it allowed Bour-
gain and Yehudayoﬀ [10] to construct explicit monotone expanders. And, in [6, 7],
Bourgain and Gamburd showed a spectral gap result in SU(d), d ≥ 2. Benoist and
Saxcé [1] extended this work to all compact simple Lie groups. Introducing the
notion of local spectral gap, Boutonnet-Ioana-Salehi Golseﬁdy [11] further gener-
alized the result to non-compact settings. Both of these generalizations build on
Saxcé’s discretized product theorem [15], whose proof uses Bourgain’s theorem in
a crucial way. Let us also mention that combining Saxcé’s product theorem with
Fourier analysis on Lie groups, Lindenstrauss-Saxcé [25] and Saxcé [16] obtained
Erdős-Volkmann type results for Borel measurable subgroups in simple Lie groups.
   In the same paper [4], Bourgain deduced from his sum-product theorem a Marstrand
type projection theorem in the discretized setting. This result is one of the main in-
gredients in the Bourgain-Furman-Lindenstrauss-Mozes theorem [5] on the equidis-
tribution for orbits of subsemigroups of SLd (Z) on the torus. This last result can
be used to study stationary measures. Namely, they obtained a stiﬀness result. It’s
worth noting that the Bourgain-Furman-Lindenstrauss-Mozes theorem was applied
by Bourgain and Varjú in [9] to show expansion in SLd (Z/qZ) with q an arbitrary
integer.

1.2. Statement of the main results. The aim of this article is to generalize
Bourgain’s discretized sum-product theorem to matrix algebras. Let E be a real
algebra of ﬁnite dimension endowed with a norm that makes it a normed vector
space. Such a structure will be called a normed algebra. We want to understand,
given a bounded subset A ⊂ E satisfying similar properties as in Theorem 1, how
Nδ (hAis ) grows and whether (1) or similar estimates hold.

Theorem 2. Let E be a normed simple real algebra of finite dimension. Given
κ > 0 and σ < dim(E), there is ǫ > 0 depending on E, κ and σ such that the
following holds for δ > 0 sufficiently small. Let A be a subset of E, assume that
      (i) A ⊂ B(0, δ −ǫ ),
     (ii) ∀ρ ≥ δ, Nρ (A) ≥ δ ǫ ρ−κ ,
    (iii) Nδ (A) ≤ δ −σ−ǫ ,
    (iv) for every proper subalgebra W ⊂ E, there is a ∈ A such that d(a, W ) ≥ δ ǫ .
Then,
(2)                   Nδ (A + A) + Nδ (A + A · A) ≥ δ −ǫ Nδ (A).
   The case E = C is due to Bourgain and Gamburd [6]. So our result is new for
dim(E) > 2. Let us also mention the work of Chang [14] who investigated the sum-
product problem for real matrices and the result of Tao in [30] concerning general
algebras, both in the discrete context.
   The assumption (ii) is a non-concentration condition. It is to avoid the situation
where A is a union of a bounded number of small balls. A subset A ⊂ E satisfying
the condition (iv) will be said to be δ ǫ -away from subalgebras. This is the additional
condition compared to the one-dimensional case. Without it A can be trapped in
a small neighborhood of a proper subalgebra. Note the conclusion (2) is slightly
weaker than that of Theorem 1. Here, instead of A · A, we need A + A · A to see
the growth. Actually, the estimate (1) fails under the same assumptions as soon as
dim(E) is greater than 1. Indeed, if A is a union of a segment of unit length and
an orthonormal basis of E, then the set A satisﬁes the assumptions2 of Theorem 2
but A + A and A · A are both unions of a bounded number of unit segments. Thus
(1) fails for such A.
   Our second result concerns linear actions on Euclidean spaces. Let X be a
bounded subset of the Euclidean space Rn . Let A ⊂ End(Rn ) be a collection of
linear endomorphisms. We can ask whether X grows under addition and transfor-
mation by elements of A, provided that A is suﬃciently rich.

1.3. Motivation. Theorem 3 is an interesting result on its own right, and we hope
it leads to fruitful applications, just like the one dimensional case did. In particular,
our work is primarily motivated by the following speciﬁc application. In [2], Benoist
and Quint have generalized the stiﬀness result of Bourgain-Furman-Lindenstrauss-
Mozes [5] to a much broader class of dynamical systems. In particular, for linear
actions on tori, they do not need the proximality assumption in [5]. However, the
results in [2] are not quantitative.
    The approach in [5] is Fourier-analytic. While a subgroup Γ ⊂ SLd (Z) acts
on the torus, its transpose t Γ acts on Fourier coeﬃcients. A large part of the
proof in [5] focuses on the study of large Fourier coeﬃcients under this action.
By the theory of random matrix products, if Γ is proximal, then large random
products in Γ behave like rank one projections composed with rotations, if viewed
at an appropriate scale. That is how Bourgain’s discretized projection theorem
comes into play. It is clear that if one wants to avoid the proximality assumption
in the Bourgain-Furman-Lindenstrauss-Mozes theorem, a higher rank discretized
projection theorem is required. And as the rank one projection theorem follows
from the sum-product theorem in R, a higher rank projection theorem can be
proved using Theorem 3. This is the subject of a subsequent paper [21].

is induced from that on Mn (R). To have a control on it, we need a quantitative
version of the Wedderburn theorem. Indeed, we show that under the quantitative
irreducibility condition (iii) of Theorem 3, the normed algebra E is isomorphic
to one of the three matrix algebras endowed with standard operator norm via a
bi-Lipschitz map with the Lipschitz constant controlled independently of A.


1.5. Organization of the paper. In Section 2 we introduce some deﬁnitions and
notations and then recall some useful tools from additive combinatorics and the
theory of semianalytic sets. Sections 3–5 prepare for the proof of the main results.
More precisely, Section 3 is dedicated to the "escape from subvariety" technique.
Section 4 deals with a lower bound on the size the set of traces. And in Section 5 we
establish an eﬀective version of the Wedderburn structure theorem. We complete
the proof of Theorem 5 and deduce Theorem 2 in Section 6. Finally Theorem 3 is
proved in Section 7 and Corollary 4 is deduced in Section 8.

                         3. Escaping from subvarieties
   In this section we show that if a subset A of a simple algebra is not trapped in
any subalgebra then we can escape from any subvariety within a bounded number
of steps using addition and multiplication. The number of necessary steps depends
only on the subvariety and the ambient algebra. This is achieved in two steps. First,
using only multiplication we can escape from linear subspaces (Proposition 16).
Then, once the set is away from linear subspaces, we can escape from subvarieties
using only addition (Lemma 17). Note that everything is quantitative. By escaping
from a subvariety we mean getting outside a neighborhood of that subvariety.
