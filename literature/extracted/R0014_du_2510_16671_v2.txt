                                                                                         1. Introduction
                                              In 1954, Marstrand [1] proved the following theorem, starting a long line of research on projection
                                           theorems.
                                           Theorem 1.1 (Marstrand [1]). Let A ⊂ R2 be a Borel measurable set, and Pt : R2 → R, t ∈ [0, 2π]
                                           be the orthogonal projection onto the span of (cos t, sin t). Then dimH Pt (A) = min(dimH A, 1) for
                                           almost every t ∈ [0, 2π].
                                              Here and in the future, we use dimH to denote Hausdorff dimension in Euclidean spaces.
                                           Marstrand’s original proof is elementary but somewhat complex. Later, Kauffman [2] provided
                                           a much simpler proof of Marstrand’s result using potential theory. His method also generalizes to
                                           higher dimensions:
                                           Theorem 1.2 (Kaufman[2]). Let A ⊂ Rn be a Borel measurable set and 1 ≤ m ≤ n, if K ∈
                                           G(n, m), and we denote the orthogonal projection from Rn onto K to be PK , then dimH PK (A) =
                                           min(dimH A, m) for almost every K ∈ G(n, m).
                                              Here G(n, m) is the Grassmannian of m dimensional subspaces of Rn . Since then, researchers
                                           have discovered various generalizations and refinements of Marstrand’s original result, and connec-
                                           tions to other problems in mathematics, e.g. the sum product theorem by Bourgain [3].
                                              Later, Gan, Guo, Guth, Harris, Maldague and Wang in [4] introduced new tools from harmonic
                                           analysis into the study of this topic. They used the so called decoupling inequalities to prove the
                                           following refinement of theorem 1.2. Using similar methods, Gan, Guo and Wang proved a further
                                           generalization in [5]:
                                           Theorem 1.3 (Restricted projection theorem [5]). Let γ : [0, 1] → Rn be a smooth curve such that
                                           det(γ(t), ..., γ (n−1) (t)) ̸= 0 for all t ∈ [0, 1] and 1 ≤ m ≤ n − 1. Let Pt be the orthogonal projection
                                           onto span(γ(t), ..., γ m (t)) and let A ⊂ Rn be Borel, then dimH Pt (A) = min(dimH A, m) for almost
                                           every t ∈ [0, 1].

   In summary, Gan, Guo, Guth, Harris, Maldague and Wang first converted the original problem
about Hausdorff dimension into trying to prove an Lp inequality about sum of indicator of paral-
lelpipeds, then they divided up the support of the Fourier transform of those indicator functions
into different regions. Each region can either be estimated directly, or be estimated using the de-
coupling inequality. Due to the form of the decoupling inequality from above, Gan, Guo and Wang
only considered families of subspaces of the form span(γ, ..., γ (k) ).
   In this paper, we ask the following question, given a family of linear maps Pt : Rn → Rm , t ∈ [0, 1],
n ≥ m, when is it true that if A ⊂ Rn is Borel, we have dimH (Pt (A)) = min(dimH A, m) for
almost every t ∈ [0, 1]? We make the following conjecture, which essentially says if the restricted
Marstrand’s theorem does not hold, then there must be counterexamples among subspaces:
Conjecture 1.5. Suppose Pt : Rn → Rm is an analytic family of linear maps, in the sense
that its coordinate functions are analytic functions of t, and assume that for any subspace π of
Rn , dim Pt (π) = min(dim π, m) for all but finitely many t ∈ [0, 1]. We have dimH Pt (A) =
min(dimH A, m) for almost every t whenever A ⊂ Rn is Borel.
   A more general version of the above conjecture is:
Conjecture 1.6. Suppose Pt : Rn → Rm is an analytic family of linear maps. Let f : [0, n] → R be
defined as follows. If x ∈ [0, n] is an integer, f (x) is the largest integer such that for any subspace
A of Rn with dim A = x, dim Pt (A) ≥ f (x) for all but finitely many t ∈ [0, 1]. If x is not an integer,
f is defined to be linear on the interval [⌊x⌋, ⌊x⌋ + 1]. We conjecture that if A ⊂ Rn is Borel, then
dimH Pt (A) ≥ f (dimH A) for almost every t.
   For example, consider the linear maps Pt : R2n 7→ Rn given by Pt ((x1 , y1 , ..., xn , yn )) = (x1 +
ty1 , ..., xn + tyn ), then we can prove that if A is Borel, dimH (Pt (A)) ≥ f (dimH A) for almost every
t. Here f (x) = ⌊ x+1   2 ⌋ if x is an integer, and f is linear between integers. We will be focusing on
the less general conjecture 1.5 in this paper.

   We are unable to prove the full conjecture above due to inefficiencies in the polynomial parti-
tioning argument at the moment when this was written. However, the recent resolution of three
dimensional Kakeya conjecture by Hong Wang and Joshua Zahl in [7] might be able to be adapted
to our case, provided that their argument for straight tubes can be generalized to curved tubes.
However, it is nontrivial to generalize their method to curved tubes, and it might involve making
their arguments work for semialgebraic sets instead of convex sets.
   We devote the rest of the paper to proving theorem 1.7. The method we use was first invented
in [8] and used again in [9]. To briefly illustrate the idea, let Pt : Rn → Rm be a family of
linear maps and A ⊂ Rn be Borel measurable, then consider the collection of curves in Rm+1
given by ca : [0, 1] 7→ Rm+1 , ca (t) = (t, Pt (a)) for each a ∈ A, then roughly speakingS to prove
that dimH Pt (A) = min(m, dimH A) for almost every t, we only need to show that dimH a∈A ca =
min(m+1, dimA +1). This problem has the same form as the curved variant of the Kakeya problem,
studied for example in [10]. In [8] and [9], the authors studied projections from R3 to R, which
correspond to unions of curves in R2 . Because of the low dimension (dimension 2), the authors
were able to study tangencies directly.
   In theorem 1.7, however, we need to deal with curves that lie in R3 , and we use the polynomial
partitioning method first invented by Larry Guth to make progress on the restriction conjecture.
We will first adapt an argument by Nets Katz and Keith Rogers in [11] to prove a version of the
polynomial Wolff axiom, and use the polynomial Partitioning method to convert it to the needed
curved Kakeya bound. Similar curved Kakeya problems have also been studied recently in [12],
where the authors applied the polynomial Wolff axioms for curves to make partial progress on a
conjecture involving Lp bounds of certain Hormander operators.
   For the rest of the proof, in section 2 we prove a version of polynomial Wolff axiom, in section 3
we use the polynomial Wolff axiom to derive a multilinear Kakeya inequality, in section 4 we use the
well known "broad narrow" method to convert the multilinear Kakeya inequality to a linear Kakeya
quality, and in the last section we discuss how to go from linear Kakeya inequality to restricted
problem.
