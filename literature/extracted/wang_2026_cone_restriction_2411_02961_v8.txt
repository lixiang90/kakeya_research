           Abstract.  We revisit the approach of Ou and Wang to the cone restriction
           problem via polynomial partitioning. By recasting their inductive scheme as a
           recursive algorithm and incorporating the nested polynomial Wolff axioms, we
           obtain improved bounds for cone restriction estimates in higher dimensions.

    Fourier restriction is connected to several central problems in harmonic analysis,
geometric measure theory, and partial differential equations. For hypersurfaces with
nonvanishing Gaussian curvature, such as the sphere and the paraboloid, these
connections include the Bochner–Riesz problem, Kakeya-type estimates, and the
Schr¨odinger maximal problem. The cone has a different geometry because one of its
principal curvatures vanishes. Nevertheless, restriction estimates for these surfaces
are closely related: the restriction conjecture for the sphere or paraboloid inRn
implies the corresponding conjecture for the cone inR   n+1 ; see [14].
    Guth [5, 6] introducedpolynomial partitioninginto Fourier restriction theory
and obtained improved estimates for the paraboloid.  Subsequent developments
include the work of Wang [19], Wang–Wu [20], Hickman–Rogers [11], Hickman–Zahl
[12], and Guo–Wang–Zhang [7]. In [11, 12], Guth’s induction-on-scales argument
was reformulated as a recursive algorithm.  This formulation permits the use of
Kakeya-type geometric estimates from [13, 25], including thenested polynomial
Wolff axioms, to control the number of tube directions compatible with a sequence
of algebraic varieties. Polynomial partitioning has also been applied to the Bochner–
Riesz problem [22, 8], the Schr¨odinger maximal problem [3], and cone restriction
[15].
    We adapt the methods of [11, 12] to the cone framework of [15]. In Section 3,
we recast the inductive argument as a recursive procedure and combine it with
the nested polynomial Wolff axioms.  The main geometric distinction from the
paraboloid argument is the scale to which a terminal wave-packet family is traced
back. At scale r, the cone has approximately r(n−2)/2          angular sectors, whereas the
paraboloid has approximately r(n−1)/2          caps. To match the cone sector count, we
trace each terminal family back to its (n−  1)-dimensional ancestor rather than to
the root.
