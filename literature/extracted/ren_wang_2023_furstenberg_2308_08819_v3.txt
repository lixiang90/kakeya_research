                                                                                        1. Introduction
                                               For s ∈ (0, 1] and t ∈ (0, 2], a (s, t)-Furstenberg set is a set E ⊂ R2 with the following property:
                                           there exists a family L of lines with dimH L ≥ t such that dimH (E ∩ ℓ) ≥ s for all ℓ ∈ L. The
                                           Hausdorff dimension dimH L is defined as the Hausdorff dimension of L in the space A(2, 1) of all
                                           affine lines in R2 (see [26, Section 3.16]). The Furstenberg set question asks: what is the minimum
                                           possible dimension of dimH (E)?
                                               Motivated by his work on ×2, ×3-invariant sets [15], Furstenberg considered a set E with the
                                           following property: there exists a family of lines L, one in every direction, such that dimH (E ∩ℓ) ≥ s
                                           for all ℓ ∈ L. He conjectured (in unpublished work, but recalled by Wolff in [43, Remark 1.5])
                                           that such a set E (which is a special (s, 1)-Furstenberg set) has Hausdorff dimension ≥ 3s+1         2 .
                                           Furstenberg’s conjecture was later generalized to (s, t)-Furstenberg sets: any (s, t)-Furstenberg set
                                           E ⊂ R2 has Hausdorff dimension
                                                                                                      3s + t
                                           (1)                                 dimH E ≥ min{s + t,           , s + 1}.
                                                                                                         2
                                           As observed by Wolff [43], the conjectured bound 3s+t     2    is optimal for the continuum version of a
                                           sharp example for Szemerédi-Trotter theorem [40]. The bound s + t for 0 ≤ t ≤ s ≤ 1 was proved
                                           by Lutz-Stull [23] and Héra-Shmerkin-Yavicoli [19], and the bound s + 1 for s + t ≥ 2 was proved
                                           recently by Fu and the first author [14]. Wolff [43] gave a sharp construction for 3s+t   2  in the case
                                           t = 1 based on a grid example (which easily generalizes to other values of t), the sharp construction
                                           for s + t can be taken as the product of a t-dim Cantor set and an s-dim Cantor set, and Fu and
                                           the first author [14] gave a sharp construction for s + 1 (the product of an interval and a s-dim
                                           Cantor set).
                                               The main case s < t < 2 − s remains open. Elementary bounds due to Wolff [43] in the case
                                           t = 1, Molter-Rela [28], Héra [18], and Lutz-Stull [23] give
                                                                             dimH E ≥ max(t/2 + s, s + min(s, t)).
                                           Meanwhile, an ε-improvement dimH (E) ≥ 2s+ ε(s, t) was proven in the special case (s, t) = (1/2, 1)
                                           by Katz-Tao [21] and Bourgain [1], then for general (s, 2s) by Héra-Shmerkin-Yavicoli [19]. Explicit
                                           improvement for (s, 2s)-Furstenberg sets was obtained by Benedetto-Zahl [7].
                                              A breakthrough by Orponen-Shmerkin [30] proves an ε-improvement over 2s for general (s, t)
                                           with s < t (see Section 1.3 for how this result leads to recent progress on several problems). Later,
                                           work of Shmerkin and the second author [38] provides an ε-improvement over the bound s + 2t using

   The (s, t)-Furstenberg problem is known to be connected to the discretized sum-product problem
and projection problem, as shown in Section 1.1, Section 1.2, and [31, Section 6].
   Recently, Wang and Wu [42] proved a “two-ends” generalization of our Theorem 1.1 and used it

1.1. Exceptional set estimate for orthogonal projections. Let K ⊂ R2 be an arbitrary Borel
set and 0 ≤ u ≤ min{dimH K, 1}. The exceptional set problem of orthogonal projections asks for
the optimal upper bound on
                                 dimH {θ ∈ S 1 : dimH πθ (K) < u}.
In 1968, Kaufman [22] proved that
                               dimH {θ ∈ S 1 : dimH πθ (K) < u} ≤ u,
sharpening a result of Marstrand [24]. In 2000, Peres-Schlag [33] showed that when dimH K > 1,
one can improve the estimate to
                   dimH {θ ∈ S 1 : dimH πθ (K) < u} ≤ max{u + 1 − dimH K, 0}.
Kaufman’s bound is sharp when u = dimH K and the Peres-Schlag bound is sharp when u = 1.
In fact, the case u = 1 was obtained previously by Falconer [11]. For general cases, Oberlin [29]
conjectured that the right-hand side should be max{2u − dimH K, 0}.
   Further improvements on the exceptional set estimate originate from Bourgain’s projection the-
orem [2], which says
(2)                       dimH {θ ∈ S 1 : dimH πθ (K) ≤ dimH K/2} = 0.
   Bourgain’s projection theorem concerns the deep nature of real numbers and has been used in
Bourgain-Furman-Lindenstrauss-Mozes [3] on random walk on torus and in Katz-Zahl [20] on the
Hausdorff dimension of Kakeya sets in R3 . Recent improvements [30, 31] on the exceptional set
estimate are associated to the improvements on the Furstenberg sets problem.
   As a corollary of Theorem 1.1 (in fact, its discretized version Theorem 4.1), we prove Oberlin’s
conjecture [29, (1.8)] on the exceptional sets of the orthogonal projections, whose AD-regular version
was established in [31, Theorem 1.13]. This deduction is analogous to the procedure used to establish
[31, Theorem 1.13] (see [31, Section 6.1]), so we omit the details.
Theorem 1.2. Let K ⊂ R2 be an arbitrary Borel set. Then for all 0 ≤ u ≤ min{dimH (K), 1}, we
have
                 dimH {θ ∈ S 1 : dimH πθ (K) < u} ≤ max{2u − dimH K, 0}.

1.2. Discretized sum-product. Let F be a field and A, B ⊂ F, the sum set A + B is defined as
                                     A + B := {a + b : a ∈ A, b ∈ B}
and the product set is defined as
                                     A · B := {a · b : a ∈ A, b ∈ B}.
The sum-product problem asks whether the size of A + A or A · A can be substantially larger
than the size of A. When A is a finite set, we measure the size of A by its cardinality and it was
conjectured by Erdös and Szemerédi that when F = R, for any ǫ > 0, there exists Cǫ > 0 such that
                                   max{|A + A|, |A · A|} ≥ Cǫ |A|2−ǫ .
See [39, 27, 34, 35, 36, 37] for some recent results on the Erdös-Szemerédi sum-product conjecture.
   When F = R, a “fractal” analogue of the discrete sum-product problem concerns the (δ, s, C)-sets,
which are discretizations of sets of Hausdorff dimension ≥ s in R. For a bounded set A ⊂ Rd , let
|A|δ denote the dyadic δ-covering number of A. This concept will be precisely defined in Definition
2.2, but for now one can regard |A|δ as being the same (up to a constant factor) as the least number
of δ-balls needed to cover A.

is called a (δ, s, C)-set if

(δ, s, CC ′ )-set.

   While the sum-product theorem can be viewed as a special case of the Furstenberg sets problem,
the asymmetric sum product, i.e. A + B · C for different sets A, B, C ⊂ [0, 1], plays an important

1.3. Roadmap. Our proof builds on previous works on projection problems and incidences for
tubes. We illustrate the relation between different results below.
        Bourgain’s discretized sum-product theorem [1]/Bourgain’s projection theorem [2]
        =⇒ ǫ-improvement of Furstenberg sets estimate [30]
        =⇒ Sharp radial projection theorem in the plane [32]
        =⇒ ABC-sum product theorem [31], [8]
        =⇒ Sharp exceptional sets estimate for almost AD-regular sets [31]
        =⇒ Sharp Furstenberg sets estimate for almost AD-regular sets [31]
        + Sharp Furstenberg sets estimate for well-spaced sets [17]
        =⇒ Sharp Furstenberg sets estimate.
   In [31], the Furstenberg conjecture is solved for almost AD-regular sets. On the other hand, in
[17], the Furstenberg conjecture is solved for well-spaced sets (see also [13] for a related result).
In this paper, we will generalize the main result in [17] and solve the Furstenberg conjecture for a
class of semi-well spaced sets. Then we combine this result with the almost AD-regular result in
an induction-on-scales scheme due to [30] to finish the proof of Furstenberg sets conjecture.
   It is interesting to note that the proof of Furstenberg sets conjecture for almost AD-regular
sets completely relies on tools from geometric measure theory and additive combinatorics, while
our result for semi-well spaced sets completely relies on Fourier analysis, specifically the high-low
method pioneered by Vinh [41] and Guth, Solomon, and the second author [17].

1.4. Structure of the paper. In Section 2, we introduce preliminaries and notations about dyadic
tubes, dimensions of sets, and the high-low method. In Section 3, we state and prove some pigeon-
holing and multiscale lemmas that will be crucial later in the proof. In Section 4, we re-prove the
special case s + t = 2 of the Furstenberg set problem, as well as introduce the key Lemma 4.8 that
will allow us to solve the Furstenberg set problem for a class of semi-well spaced sets. In Section
5, we prove Lemma 4.8 using a high-low method. Finally, in Section 6, we prove a decomposition
lemma for Lipschitz functions that reduces the full Furstenberg set conjecture to the AD-regular
and semi-well spaced cases.
