                                           1. Introduction

   This paper and the companion paper of Orponen and Shmerkin [30] study dual Furstenberg sets
and restricted projections when the families of planes satisfy a local non-concentration condition with
respect to pencils at most point and scales, which we call the spread-out condition. The companion
paper introduces spread-outness and proves a sharp Furstenberg estimate in the “sticky case”, where
the point set is “almost Ahlfors-David regular”. Our main task is to remove this regularity assumption,
which is achieved in full generality in Theorems 1.6 and 1.7.
   The two papers form a single project. We present them separately to clarify the exposition and to
distinguish the authorship of the sticky estimate from that of the passage to general configurations.
We repeat some introductory material from [30] to keep this paper self-contained.

1.1. Restricted projections. For V ∈ G(d, k), let ΠV : Rd → V denote orthogonal projection onto
V . The Marstrand-Mattila projection theorem [25, 26] says that almost every projection has the
largest possible dimension. More precisely, if E ⊂ Rd is Borel and γd,k denotes Lebesgue measure on
the Grassmannian G(d, k) of k-planes in Rd , then
(1.1)                dimH ΠV (E) = min{dimH E, k}         for γd,k -a.e. V ∈ G(d, k).
What happens if the planes are chosen from a much smaller family? In projection theory, one
studies dimH ΠV (E) for ν-typical V , where ν is a highly singular measure on G(d, k), sometimes with
additional assumptions on E. See [42] for a survey, including connections with the Kakeya problem.
   We ask which measures ν can replace γd,k in (1.1). Much is known when the measure is length
measure on a smooth curve in G(d, k); see, for example, [12, 21, 33, 16] for G(3, 1), [12, 14] for G(3, 2),
and [15, 9, 42] for higher-dimensional Grassmannians and further references. For smooth curves, the
picture is fairly complete in dimension and co-dimension 1 (k = 1 or d − 1). Much less is known for
1 < k < d − 1. These proofs rely on smoothness, and it is not clear how to extend them to other
measures. Chen [6] took a different approach, proving a restricted projection theorem for a class of
random measures on G(3, 1). His argument does not extend to G(2, 1).
  In particular, no genuine restricted Marstrand-type theorem was known for measures on G(2, 1).
Our first result applies to uniformly perfect measures of directions. A locally finite Borel measure
µ on a metric space X is uniformly perfect if diam(spt µ) > 0 and there are β ∈ [0, 1) and D > 1
                   SPREAD-OUT FURSTENBERG SETS II: GENERAL CONFIGURATIONS                              3


such that µ(B(x, r)) ≤ β · µ(B(x, Dr)) for every closed ball B(x, r) ⊂ X with spt µ ̸⊂ B(x, Dr).
Every non-trivial Ahlfors s-regular measure with s > 0 is uniformly perfect. (A measure µ on a
metric space X is Ahlfors s-regular if there is C ≥ 1 such that C −1 rs ≤ µ(B(x, r)) ≤ Crs for all
x ∈ spt µ, r ∈ (0, diam(spt µ)].)

Theorem 1.1. Let ν be a uniformly perfect measure on G(2, 1). For any Borel set E ⊂ R2 ,

                      dimH ΠV (E) = min{dimH E, 1}        for ν-a.e. V ∈ G(2, 1).

   Kaufman and Mattila [22] constructed singular measures ν on G(2, 1), of dimension arbitrarily
close to 1, for which the Marstrand-Mattila estimate fails for some compact set E and all directions
in spt(ν). Both E and ν have a special structure. In particular, ν is supported on a “well-spaced”
set Θ: for many pairs of scales 0 < δ ≪ r, the set Θ ∩ Br is contained in a δ-ball for every r-ball Br .
Uniform perfectness rules out these gaps between scales.
   In higher dimensions, there are simple linear obstructions to a Marstrand-Mattila estimate. Namely,
for W ∈ G(d, d − k), consider the Schubert cycle

                                 SW = {V ∈ G(d, k) : V ∩ W ̸= {0}}.

Then
                 dimH (ΠV (W ⊥ )) ≤ k − 1 < k = min{dim W ⊥ , k}      for all V ∈ SW .

In particular, Ahlfors-regularity is not enough to guarantee a Marstrand-Mattila estimate for any
d ≥ 3 and arbitrary k. Schubert cycles are essentially the same as the pencils used in projection
theory, see [42, Definition 1.4].
   The Kaufman-Mattila example also shows why a global non-concentration assumption is not
enough. In G(2, 1), the Schubert cycles are points. A well-spaced set avoids concentration at any one
point globally, but can be concentrated near a point at many scales. Theorem 1.2 shows that a mild
local non-concentration near Schubert cycles at every scale suffices for a Marstrand-Mattila estimate.

Theorem 1.2. Let 1 ≤ k < d. Let ν be a nonzero finite Borel measure on G(d, k). Assume that, for
some ε ∈ (0, 1/2),
                                                  

for all V ∈ spt ν, W ∈ G(d, d − k), and 0 < r ≤ 1. Then, for any Borel set E ⊂ Rd ,

                      dimH ΠV (E) = min{dimH E, k}        for ν-a.e. V ∈ G(d, k).

In particular, for any 1 ≤ k < d, there are measures supported on sets of arbitrarily small (positive)

   He [18] proved a higher-dimensional projection theorem under a global (rather than local) power-
law non-concentration near Schubert cycles, with a dimension bound strictly above (k/d) dimH E for
0 < dimH E < d; this is a higher dimensional version of Bourgain’s projection theorem [5]. Under the
stronger local non-concentration assumption (1.2), we obtain the optimal Marstrand-Mattila estimate.
   We prove these results through discretized estimates, which are also useful in applications. Theorem
1.7 is the discretized version of Theorem 1.2.

1.4. Applications. Theorem 1.6 has applications to smooth restricted projections, distance sets,
additive combinatorics, and self-affine sets. Section 4 contains the proofs and further discussion.

1.4.1. Marstrand for surfaces in G(4, 2). Although our motivation comes from fractal restricted pro-
jections and Furstenberg sets, Theorem 1.6 also applies to smooth submanifolds of G(d, k). For
G(4, 2), combining the theorem with the results of [15], we obtain a restricted projection theorem for
generic surfaces.

Theorem 1.8. Let M be the closed unit ball in R2 . There is an open and dense set U of embeddings
F : M → G(4, 2) in the compact-open C ∞ topology such that, for all F ∈ U and for any Borel set
E ⊂ R4 ,
                   dimH (ΠF (x) (E)) = min{dimH (E), 2} for H2 -a.e. x ∈ M.

    See Section 4.3 for the proof and context.

1.4.2. Pinned distance projections. Given a set E ⊂ Rd and y ∈ Rd , let ∆y (E) = {|x − y| : x ∈ E}
denote the pinned distance set of E with respect to y. A simple reduction from pinned distance
projections to orthogonal projections allows us to apply Theorem 1.7: if E ⊂ Rd is Borel and ν is a
spread-out measure on Rd , then

See §4.4 for the simple proof and discussion.

1.4.3. Additive combinatorics and fractals. It is well-known that projection and Furstenberg estimates
lead to additive combinatorial estimates for fractal sets [5]. Extending [30, Theorem 6.5], we obtain
a discretized sum-product estimate for spread-out sets satisfying a Katz–Tao condition. See §4.5 for
the statement, proof, and further discussion.

1.4.4. A new proof of the Bárány-Hochman-Rapaport theorem. The Ledrappier-Young formula and
more general versions connect projection theorems to the dimension theory of dynamical systems. As
a direct corollary, we recover the main result of [2]: the Hausdorff dimension of a planar, strongly
separated self-affine set equals its affinity dimension, provided that the linear parts of the defining
affine maps generate a non-compact and totally irreducible subgroup of GL(2, R). See §4.6 for further
discussion.

1.5. Proof strategy. The starting point in the proof of Theorem 1.6 is the sticky Furstenberg
estimate in the companion paper, [30, Theorem 1.9], where P is “almost Ahlfors-regular”. We recall
the definitions from [30] in Section 2. Ren and Wang [35, §6] similarly passed from regular to general
planar Furstenberg configurations by decomposing branching functions and combining estimates at
different scales, although the details differ substantially.
  Our argument to reduce the general case to the sticky case has several parallels to the proof of the
3D Kakeya theorem [45, 17] and it is significantly simpler, but there are some important differences.
We establish the estimate by induction on the loss δ η : we show that an estimate with loss δ η implies
                         ′
an estimate with loss δ η for some η ′ < η.
   The sticky theorem handles configurations whose branching function is approximately linear. Oth-
erwise, there is an intermediate scale ρ at which the point set has excess branching, |P |ρ ≥ ρ−(d−k)−χ
for some positive χ.
    We use this excess branching to improve the exponent and close the induction. After some rescaling
arguments inspired by [8, 44], we are led to studying configurations of slablets. A priori we know very
little about their branching function, but we show that we can reduce to one of two cases: the slablets
satisfy a Frostman condition with error δ −ε for a small but fixed ε ≪ ρ, or they satisfy a Katz–Tao
condition at a suitable scale. In the Frostman case, the gain comes from the excess branching at scale
ρ; in the Katz–Tao case, it comes from the subcritical number of slablets that arises from the failure
of the Frostman condition with error δ −ε . In both cases, we can close the induction and obtain a
better exponent η ′ < η.

Theorem 4.10 (Gan–Guo–Wang). Fix 1 ≤ k < d. Let γ : [0, 1] → Rd be a smooth non-degenerate
curve, that is, the vectors γ ′ (t), . . . , γ (d) (t) are linearly independent for all t ∈ [0, 1]. Let Vt ∈ G(d, k)
be the k-dimensional subspace spanned by γ ′ (t), . . . , γ (k) (t). Then, for any Borel set E ⊂ Rd with
dimH (E) ≤ k,
                          dimH (ΠVt (E)) = min{dimH (E), k}          for a.e. t ∈ [0, 1].

   For k ∈ {1, d − 1}, this family is generic in the space of smooth curves in G(d, k). For intermediate
values of k, it is very special. Understanding which curves satisfy a Marstrand-type theorem remains
a difficult open problem, even for polynomial curves; see [9, 42]. Some progress for polynomial curves
in G(4, 2) appears in [9].
   For smooth surfaces in G(4, 2), we prove a Marstrand-type theorem by combining two ideas. Elliptic
surfaces are spread out. Hyperbolic surfaces satisfying an additional derivative condition are locally
foliated by curves to which Theorem 4.10 applies. These two cases cover a generic surface except
for a set of zero area. By comparison, generic 3-manifolds in the four-dimensional space G(4, 2)
can be treated by the classical transversality method [31], although this fact does not seem to be
recorded in the literature (the special case of isotropic planes in G(4, 2) was established by Hovila [20,
Theorem 1.1] using transversality). That method breaks down in dimensions less than 3, or less than
d − 1 in G(d, k).
   A big-cell chart represents a plane in G(4, 2) by a 2 × 2 matrix. In this chart, a smooth surface Σ
has a local parametrization F (u, v) with linearly independent partial derivatives. At V = F (u, v), its
tangent plane is TV Σ = span{∂u F, ∂v F } ⊂ R2×2 . Keep the two planes distinct: V is a plane in R4 ,
while TV Σ consists of the possible first derivatives of curves on Σ through V .
   The function (a, b) 7→ det(a∂u F + b∂v F ) is a quadratic form in two variables. We call V elliptic
if this form is definite (strictly positive or strictly negative away from the origin), hyperbolic if it
is indefinite (it takes both signs), and parabolic if it is degenerate (a scalar multiple of the square
of a linear form, including the zero form). At an elliptic point every nonzero tangent matrix is
invertible. At a hyperbolic point the determinant vanishes along exactly two lines in TV Σ; these are
the characteristic directions, and their nonzero matrices have rank one. Changes of big-cell chart act

on tangent matrices by invertible left and right multiplication. They multiply the determinant by a
nonzero factor, so the classification does not depend on the chart.

of dimension at most one. Since F is Lipschitz on M , H2 (F (Z)) = 0. If p ∈ M \ Z is elliptic,
ellipticity holds in a neighborhood U of p. If p is hyperbolic, both maps in (4.15) are nonzero along
each characteristic direction throughout a neighborhood U of p. Proposition 4.12 therefore applies to
both characteristic foliations of F (U ). Cover M \ Z by countably many coordinate patches satisfying
one of these two conditions. Propositions 4.11 and 4.12, together with H2 (F (Z)) = 0, establish the
projection conclusion.                                                                              □
Remark 4.14. Propositions 4.11 and 4.12 have an interpretation in terms of the pencils PW from
[42, Definition 1.4]. For a fixed line K inside a fixed three-plane H, the family {V ′ : K ⊂ V ′ ⊂ H} is
called a Grassmannian line: choosing V ′ amounts to choosing a line in the two-dimensional quotient
H/K. If A : V → R4 /V has rank one, set K = ker A and define H ⊃ V by H/V = im A. Then A
is tangent to both PK ⊥ = {V ′ : K ⊂ V ′ } and PH ⊥ = {V ′ : V ′ ⊂ H}, and to their intersection, the
Grassmannian line just defined. Conversely, every nonzero tangent direction to a Grassmannian line
has rank one.
   Thus an elliptic tangent plane contains no direction tangent to a Grassmannian line. Equivalently,
the surface is transverse to every pencil PW through V with dim W ∈ {1, 3}: the tangent spaces of
the surface and the pencil together span TV G(4, 2). The surface is also transverse to every pencil
PW with dim W = 2 at its smooth points. These transversality statements express geometrically the
nonvanishing tangential derivatives underlying Proposition 4.11.
   A hyperbolic tangent plane instead contains exactly two directions tangent to Grassmannian lines.
Along a characteristic leaf, K(t) and W (t) determine the pencils PK(t)⊥ and PW (t)⊥ . The two maps
in (4.15) test whether K(t) and W (t), respectively, change to first order. If K(t) is locally constant,
the leaf is contained in a fixed pencil PK(t)⊥ ; if W (t) is locally constant, it is contained in a fixed
pencil PW (t)⊥ . Hence Theorem 4.13 says that, outside an H2 -null set, a generic surface either has no
tangent pencil direction or has two such directions along whose characteristic leaves both associated
pencils change to first order.

contract uniformly on the projective line, so the Furstenberg measure is self-conformal. Irreducibility
ensures that it is not supported on a single point. By Corollary 4.4, ν is spread out. Theorem 4.7
now yields (4.18) for the self-affine measure µ. Applying the positive subsystem approximation of [28,
Theorem 1.1], together with the Ledrappier–Young formula, we recover [2, Theorem 1.1].

systems, and Rapaport [34] extended them to higher dimensions. The general problem remains open
for d ≥ 4. Our method has an obstacle in higher dimensions: the Furstenberg measures are invariant
under non-conformal dynamics and can concentrate locally near hyperplanes, so spread-outness can
fail.
