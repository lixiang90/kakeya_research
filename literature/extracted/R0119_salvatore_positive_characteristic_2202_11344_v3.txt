                                                    The Kakeya Conjecture on Local Fields of Positive
                                                                    Characteristic
                                                                                       Alejo Salvatore
arXiv:2202.11344v3 [math.NT] 2 Sep 2022




                                                                                           Abstract
                                                    We state and prove an analogue of the Kakeya conjecture for the local field Fq ((t)). This
                                                extends Arsovski’s result on the Kakeya conjecture to local fields of positive characteristic. We
                                                also prove the Kakeya maximal conjecture in this setting.


                                          1     Introduction
                                          A Besicovitch set or Kakeya set is defined as a compact set E ⊆ Rn that contains a unit line segment
                                          in every direction. The Kakeya conjecture states that every Besicovitch set in Rn has Hausdorff
                                          dimension n. This problem has been thoroughly researched due to its connections with harmonic
                                          analysis, differential equations, arithmetic combinatorics and number theory, cf. [Tao01; Bou00;
                                          Wol99]. The conjecture remains open for n ≥ 3. Let d(n) denote the least possible value for the
                                          Hausdorff dimension of a Besicovitch set in Rn . Currently the best lower bounds for n = 3, 4 are
                                          established in [KZ19] and [KZ21], where the authors show that d(3) ≥ 52 + ε0 and d(4) ≥ 3.059,
                                          respectively. For n = 6, the best known bound is d(n) ≥ 4, proved by Wolff in [Wol95]. For the
                                          best known results in higher dimensions, see [KT02], [HRZ19] and [Zah21]. In regard to Minkowski
                                          dimension, Katz and Tao [KT02] showed that every Besicovitch set in Rn has Minkowski dimension
                                                   n
                                          at least α + α−1α where α ≈ 1.675.
                                              In view of the difficulty of this conjecture, Wolff proposed in [Wol99] a finite field analogue of
                                          the Kakeya conjecture as a toy model for the real case. He asked whether it was possible to find
                                          constants Cn > 0 such that if Fq is the finite field with q elements, then every Kakeya set E ⊆ Fnq has
                                          at least Cn q n elements. This was solved affirmatively by Dvir in his influential paper [Dvi09] using
                                                                                                                 1
                                          the polynomial method. His original proof gave the value Cn = n!         , which was subsequently improved
                                                     −n                              1−n
                                          to Cn = 2      in [Dvi+13] and to Cn = 2        in [BC21]. However, it was noted in [EOT10] that the
                                          analogy between the classical and finite field Kakeya conjectures is flawed in two ways. Firstly, the
                                          bound obtained in [Dvi09] is too strong, as it says that all Kakeya sets in Fnq have, in a sense, positive
                                          measure. In contrast, there are Besicovitch sets in Rn which have Lebesgue measure zero. Another
                                          deficiency of Fq is that it does not admit “multiple scales”: that is, there is no natural notion of
                                          distance on this ring that is nontrivial. These problems are related to each other, since the existence
                                          of multiple scales in Rn is what allows for the construction of Besicovitch sets with measure zero. For
                                          this reason, in [EOT10], Ellenberg, Oberlin and Tao proposed analogues of the Kakeya conjecture for
                                          metric rings that have multiple scales, specifically local fields, their rings of integers and quotient rings.
                                          The question of proving local field variants of the Kakeya conjecture had already been investigated
                                          in the 1990s by J. Wright, who gave a series of lectures in the University of New South Wales on this
                                          topic.
                                              In [DH13], Dummit and Hablicsek contructed Kakeya sets of measure zero in Fq ((t))n for all n ≥ 2,
                                          and they also showed that Kakeya sets in Fq [[t]]2 or Z2p have Minkowski dimension 2. Fraser [Fra16]
                                          constructed Kakeya sets of measure zero in Ln , where L is any non-archimedean local field with finite
                                          residue field. Hickman and Wright [HW18] studied the Kakeya conjecture on Z/N Z in relation with
                                          the discrete restriction conjecture, and they gave a simpler construction of a Kakeya set in Znp of


                                                                                                1
measure zero. Caruso [Car18] has shown that almost all Kakeya needle sets have measure zero in the
non-archimedean case. These results show that non-archimedean local fields behave in a similar way
to R in regard to Kakeya sets.
   Recently Arsovski [Ars21] proved the Kakeya conjecture for the p-adic field Qp using the polynomial
method. This result implies the Kakeya conjecture over any finite field extension L/Qp , so the Kakeya
conjecture holds for any non-archimedean local field of characteristic zero. In this article we will adapt
Arsovski’s proof to the case of local fields of positive characteristic. The main result of this article is
the following:
Theorem 1. All c-Kakeya sets in Fq ((t))n have Hausdorff dimension n.
    In order to do this one needs to find an appropriate completely ramified extension of Fq ((t)) that is
analogous to the extension of Qp given by adjoining a primitive pk -th root of unity. This is achieved
using well-known properties of Lubin–Tate extensions.

With this norm K becomes a complete, locally compact metric group with Hausdorff dimension n.

    Given a field F with an absolute value | · | satisfying (2), we define OF = {x ∈ F : |x| ≤ 1}, which
is called the valuation domain, and mF = {x ∈ F : |x| < 1}. It is easy to show that OF is a subring
of F and that mF is an ideal of OF . Note that for every x ∈ OF with |x| = 1, its inverse x−1 ∈ F is

also contained in OF . Since every nonzero element of the quotient ring OF /mF is represented by an
element x ∈ OF of absolute value 1, it follows that OF /mF is a field, and it is called the residue field
of F . In our case of interest F = K, we have OK = A and mK = tA, so the residue field is A/tA ∼   = Fq .
A non-archimedean local field can be defined as a field F with an absolute value | · | satisfying (2),
that is complete as a metric space and such that its residue field OF /mF is a finite field. Thus Fq ((t))
is a local field for every prime power q. The other main example of a local field is the field Qp of
p-adic numbers, where p is a prime number. It is defined as the quotient field of the ring

                                           Zp = Z[[t]]/(t − p),

which is an integral domain. The elements of Zp are called p-adic integers. It can be shown (cf.
[Wei74, Chapter 1] or [Jac89, Thm. 9.16]) that every non-archimedean local field is a finite extension
of either Fp [[t]] or Qp , for some prime p. Conversely, for every finite separable extension L of either
Fp [[t]] or Qp , it is possible to extend the absolute value of the base field uniquely so that L becomes a
local field (cf. [Jac89, §9.8]).
     Given 0 < c ≤ 1, we define a c-Kakeya set in K n as a compact set E ⊆ K n such that for any
direction w ∈ Fq [[t]]n there is a set Jw ⊆ Fq [[t]] of measure ≥ c and bw ∈ K n such that bw + Jw .w ⊆ E.

2    Overview of the proof
Both Arsovki’s article and this one use Dvir’s polynomial method, which can be succintly described
in the following way: given a Kakeya set E of small size, find a nonzero polynomial g of low degree
that vanishes on E; then use the fact that E contains lines in many different directions to show that
either g or a related polynomial is zero, arriving at a contradiction.
    In order to find the polynomial, Dvir’s original argument used the following Lemma whose proof
is based on dimension counting:
                                                                                                         
Lemma 2. Let k, n, d > 0 be integers. Let F be a field, and let S ⊆ F n be a set with less than n+d   n
elements. Then there is a nonzero polynomial g ∈ F [X1 , . . . , Xn ] of degree ≤ d such that g(s) = 0 for
all s ∈ S.
    Afterwards, a method of multiplicities [Dvi+13] was developed, which requires a polynomial that
vanishes with high multiplicity at each point of E, and obtains better lower bounds in the finite field
case. However, the proof of Theorem 1 does not require a stronger result than Lemma 2.
    It is possible to use the polynomial method for local fields since the problem of bounding Hausdorff
dimension can be discretized. As we shall see in the next section, the proof of Theorem 1 essentially
boils down down to showing that every Kakeya set E ⊆ (A/tk A)n has at least q kn /u(k, n) elements,
where u(k, n) .ε q kε for every ε > 0. By treating R = A/tk A as an Fq -vector space of dimension k
and using lower bounds from the finite field case, one can show that |E| ≥ 2−kn q kn . However, this is
essentially the best bound obtainable by applying the polynomial method over finite fields, since there
are examples (cf. [SS08]) of Kakeya sets in Fkn q with & 2
                                                               −kn kn
                                                                   q elements. One problem with this
approach is that polynomials with coefficients in Fq normally do not distinguish between an R-line
and an arbitrary affine subspace of dimension k over Fq . A subexponential bound for u(k, n) was
proved for n = 2 in [DH13], but the argument seemingly cannot be generalized to higher dimensions.
    Arsovski’s innovation consists of working with a primitive pk -th root of unity ζ in an algebraic
closure of Qp and taking advantage of the group isomorphism ζ Z ∼    = Z/pk Z to map the Kakeya set into
               Z n           n
a subset of (ζ ) ⊆ Qp (ζ) . Arsovski then applies the polynomial method on Qp (ζ)n . This solves the
aforementioned problem, and has the added benefit of preserving some of the algebraic structure of
E. This is because every line in Rn gets mapped into a translate of a subgroup isomorphic to Z/pk Z.

    This strategy is not viable over fields of characteristic p > 0, since the only p-th root of unity in
these fields is 1. However, the multiplicative group ζ Z can be reinterpreted as the orbit of ζ under
the action of the Galois group Gal(Qp (ζ)/Qp ), and this idea also works in positive characteristic.
The Galois group needs to be isomorphic to R = A/tk A. Fortunately, the Existence Theorem in
Local Class Field Theory tells us that for every open subgroup H ⊆ K × of finite index there is a
corresponding abelian extension L/K with Galois group Gal(L/K) ∼              = K × /H. Since the additive
            k                                            ×
group A/t A can be realized as a quotient of K /H (for instance taking H = (1 + tk A) tZ ), this
gives us the desired extension L/K. It is easy to show that L must necessarily be totally ramified.
For this type of abelian extensions, Lubin–Tate theory gives a very concrete description of the Galois
action. More precisely, this action can be realized as the quotient of an A-action on L by power series.
Moreover, there is a polynomial fk whose splitting field is L, such that its sets of roots Λk is an
additive group, and the elements of A acts as group endomorphisms, which makes Λk an A-module.
Addition allows for “lines” in Λnk passing through the origin to be translated.
    There is also a slight modification in the last part of the argument which arrives at a contradiction,
in relation to the finite field case. In Dvir’s argument, after homogenizing the polynomial so that it
can be evaluated in the projective space Pn (Fq ), one uses degree considerations to conclude that the
homogenization vanishes at the hyperspace at infinity, which corresponds to the set of directions of
affine lines in Fq . In terms of the original polynomial, this means that its homogeneous component of
highest degree vanishes, so applying the Schwartz-Zippel Lemma yields a contradiction. In Arsovski’s
method one works over a finite extension L of the local field. In this setting, we shall reduce the
coefficients of the polynomial modulo the maximal ideal mL (which can be understood as “evaluating
at t = 0”) to obtain a polynomial g ∈ B[z1 , . . . , zn ], where B = Fq [X], that takes small values at many
points y ∈ C n , where C n ⊆ B n is a set that is in one-to-one correspondence with the set of directions
of Rn . Thus C n is an analogue of the hyperspace at infinity, and the Schwartz-Zippel Lemma will be
replaced by Lemma 7.

Remark. It is possible to use Lubin–Tate polynomials to prove the Kakeya conjecture for p-adic
fields, although some adjustments have to be made. In first place, one can no longer take f to
be an additive polynomial, since the only additive polynomials in characteristic zero are the linear
polynomials f (X) = aX with constant term equal to zero. However, it can be shown that if f ∈ Qp [X]
satisfies f (X) ≡ pX (mod X 2 ) and f (X) ≡ X p (mod p), then there is a unique formal group law
F (X, Y ) ∈ Qp [[X, Y ]] such that f (F (X, Y )) = F (f (X), f (Y )). Instead of the additive property,
the map [ · ]f satisfies [a + b]f = F ([a]f , [b]f ) for all a, b ∈ Zp . Thus one should choose f so that the
corresponding power series F (X, Y ) and [a]f take a relatively simple form. A sensible choice is given by
f (X) = (1+X)p −1, whose associated formal group law is F (X, Y ) = X +Y +XY = (1+X)(1+Y )−1.
                               k
The roots of f ◦k = (1 + X)p − 1 are of the form 1 + ζ j , where 0 ≤ j < pk and ζ is a primitive pk -th
root of unity. Therefore, using this choice of f one recovers Arsovski’s proof.
