  The main theorem of [18] is the following version of the Kakeya conjecture which says that µ(T)
can only be large when the density of T in some convex set is large.
Theorem 1.2. (Wang-Zahl, [18]) If T is a set of δ-tubes in R3 , and ∆max (T) ⪅ 1, then

                                                 µ(T) ⪅ 1.
   This is the Wolff axioms version of the Kakeya conjecture, which easily implies Conjecture 1.1
for n = 3. Given Theorem 1.2, a simple sampling argument shows that if T is any set of δ-tubes in
R3 , then µ(T) ⪅ ∆max (T).
   The proof in [18] builds on important contributions by Wolff, Bourgain, Katz, Laba, and Tao.
First we will describe these older contributions. These contributions were interesting and striking,
but a proof of the Kakeya conjecture still looked very difficult. Second we describe the situation
before the recent work and discuss the remaining difficulties. Then we describe the new contribution
in [18].
   The goal of this survey is to describe the general strategy of the proofs, and we will leave out
technical details such as pigeonholing arguments and keeping careful track of small parameters.

                          2. A key obstacle: the Heisenberg group
   We begin our story with one of the key obstacles. There are cousin problems that sound quite
similar to the Kakeya conjecture but behave differently. The Heisenberg group example, introduced
by Katz-Laba-Tao in [7], is an important example of this type. It concerns the complex analogue
of the Kakeya problem with convex Wolff axioms.
   We define a complex tube in Cn with radius δ and length r by taking a complex line in Cn ,
intersecting it with a ball of radius r, and then taking its δ-neighborhood. We define volume on Cn
by identifying it with R2n , so if T is a δ-tube in Cn of length 1, then |T | ∼ δ 2(n−1) . The quantities
∆(T, K) and ∆max (T) can be defined as above.
   In C3 , there is a set of complex δ-tubes T with ∆max (T) ⪅ 1 and µ(T) ≈ δ −1 . This example
shows that the complex analogue of Theorem 1.2 is false. This example is called the Heisenberg
group example. It is based on a quadratic real algebraic hypersurface in C3 . There are a couple
choices for this hypersurface. One is the surface H defined by

                              H = {(z1 , z2 , z3 : |z1 |2 + |z2 |2 − |z3 |2 = 1}
This hypersurface contains many complex lines. For example, if α is a unit complex number, then
the line defined by z1 = 1, z3 = αz2 lies in H. All these lines pass through the point (1, 0, 0) ∈ H.
The surface H is very symmetric (it is symmetric under the action of the group U (2, 1)). By
homogeneity there are infinitely many lines thru every point of H. Taking δ-neighorhoods of these
lines gives a set of tubes T where ∆max (T) ⪅ 1 and yet µ(T) ≈ δ −1 . Ruling out examples like this
one will be a key part of our story.

   The “sticky” case is an important special case of the Kakeya problem. Because ∆max (T) ⪅ 1,

we see that for every Tρ , |TTρ | ⪅ ρδ2 . A Kakeya set is called sticky if this upper bound is roughly
tight for all ρ and all Tρ ∈ Tρ . In other words, a Kakeya set is sticky if the tubes of T pack into
fatter tubes Tρ as much as they are allowed to do so given that ∆max (T) ⪅ 1.
   If T is near-worst-case among sticky Kakeya sets, then it must have a some rigid structure. Recall
that U (T) denotes ∪T ∈T T . Suppose that x ∈ U (T). Typically, x belongs to ≈ µ(T) tubes T ∈ T.

(1)                  µ(T) ≈ µ(TTρ ) · #{Tρ ∈ Tρ : x ∈ U (TTρ )} ≤ µ(TTρ )µ(Tρ ).
   Define γsticky to be the infimal number so that µ(T) ⪅ δ −γsticky whenever ∆max (T) ⪅ 1 and
T is sticky. Suppose that we are looking at a nearly-worst-case sticky Kakeya set, so we have
µ(T) ≈ δ −γsticky . After a linear coordinate transformation, TTρ can be thought of as a set of δ/ρ
tubes. Since ∆max (T) ⪅ 1, it follows that ∆max (TTρ ) ⪅ 1 as well. Next, since ∆(TTρ , Tρ ) ≈ 1, it
follows that ∆max (Tρ ) ⪅ 1 as well. And by the definition of sticky, TTρ and Tρ are both sticky
also. So all together we have

                δ −γsticky ≈ µ(T) ⪅ µ(TTρ )µ(Tρ ) ⪅ (δ/ρ)−γsticky ρ−γsticky = δ −γsticky .
   We see that the inequalities above must be approximate equalities. In particular, the inequality
in (1) must be approximately equality. This gives some striking information! It tells us that for a
typical point x in our Kakeya set,

                                 #{Tρ ∈ Tρ : x ∈ U (TTρ )} ≈ µ(Tρ ).
   Consider a point x ∈ U (T) and look at B(x, ρ). The ball B(x, ρ) lies in ≈ µ(Tρ ) ≈ ρ−γsticky
fat tubes Tρ ∈ Tρ . For most of these Tρ , x ∈ U (TTρ ) ∩ B(x, ρ). That means that the sets
U (TTρ ) ∩ B(x, ρ) must overlap almost perfectly, as Tρ varies among the many tubes in Tρ that pass
through B(x, ρ).
   Developing these ideas, Katz, Laba, and Tao showed in [7] that such a sticky Kakeya set must
have many remarkable structural properties. For instance, U (T) ∩ B(δ 1/2 ) must consist of a union
of δ × δ 1/2 × δ 1/2 plates, called grains (like the grains in wood). And all the grains within B(δ 1/2 )
must be parallel to each other, which is called plaininess.
   The Heisenberg group example in C3 is sticky, plainy, and grainy, as explained in [7]. So we have
seen that any sticky Kakeya set must have a great deal of structure in common with the Heisenberg
group. The existence of the Heisenberg group is related to the fact that R is a subring of C, and
the Hausdorff dimension of R is half that of C.
   Katz and Tao developed a strategy to prove that any sticky Kakeya set in R3 with large µ(T)
must be associated to something like a fractal approximate subring of R. This would be a fractal
subset A ⊂ R which is approximately closed under both addition and multiplication. Bourgain’s
discretized sum product theorem in [1] shows that no such set exists. This strategy would then
show that any sticky Kakeya set must have µ(T) ⪅ 1.
   Katz and Tao did not publish a paper with their strategy, but Tao put an outline on his blog
[15]. In [17], Wang and Zahl fleshed out this outline (overcoming significant technical issues), and
proved that every sticky Kakeya set obeys the Kakeya conjecture.
Theorem 3.1. (Sticky Kakeya, [17]) If T obeys ∆max (T) ⪅ 1 and if |TTρ | ≈ (ρ/δ)2 for every ρ
and every Tρ ∈ Tρ , then

                                              µ(T) ⪅ 1.

The proof of the sticky Kakeya theorem in [17] uses some important recent developments in pro-
jection theory that build on Bourgain’s discretized sum-product theorem, especially [11] and [13].
   This work shows a remarkable connection between the sticky case of the Kakeya problem and
mathematical structures like the Heisenberg group, subrings of R, and sum-product inequalities. It
is certainly interesting mathematics. But it was not so clear how much progress these results make
towards the general Kakeya conjecture. Is the sticky case a crucial case? Or is it just a rare special
case?


                                    4. Sticky vs. not sticky
    Should we expect the “worst” Kakeya set to be sticky? Can we reduce the general problem to
the sticky case?
    On the one hand, it might feel intuitive that the ‘sticky’ case is the worst. If we are trying to
arrange tubes to overlap a lot, then perhaps we ought to put them close to each other as much as
possible. If we consider a set of tubes T without the restriction ∆max (T) ⪅ 1, then it is easy to
arrange that µ(T) is large by packing many tubes into a convex set. Since the condition ∆(T, K) ⪅ 1
is limiting us from arranging a lot of overlap, it may be intuitive that we should pack tubes into
convex sets as much as we are allowed to.
    Analysts have considered many cousins of the Kakeya problem. For many years, the worse known
example for every cousin problem was sticky. In [2] Bourgain considered a variation of the Kakeya
problem for curved tubes in R3 . In this curved version, the smallest possible volume of |U (T)| is
∼ δ, and the worst case example is sticky. In [7], Katz, Laba, and Tao gave the Heisenberg group
example, which showed that the analogue of the Kakeya problem with convex Wolff axioms is false
in C3 . The Heisenberg group example is sticky. In [16], Tao noticed that the Kakeya problem with
convex Wolff axioms is also false in R4 . The worst case example comes from a degree 2 algebraic
hypersurface, and it is also sticky. It seemed plausible that for a broad class of problems of this
type, the worst case is sticky.

   However, it has been difficult to turn this intuition into a rigorous proof. Katz and Tao and
others wondered whether the general Kakeya problem could be reduced to the sticky case, but
they didn’t see any way to do it. In 2017, in [10], Orponen proved the sticky case of the Falconer
conjecture, another longstanding problem in geometric measure theory which is a kind of cousin
of the Kakeya problem. This remarkable proof had a lot of influence in the field, but no one has
managed to reduce the general Falconer conjecture to the sticky case.
   In 2019 in [8], Katz and Zahl found a new cousin of the Kakeya problem, and gave evidence
that the worse case example is not sticky. They considered the Wolff axioms version of the Kakeya
problem over a different ring – they replaced R by the ring A = Fp [x]/(x2 ). The ring A has a
natural notion of distance with two distinct length scales. There is a cousin of the Heisenberg
group in A3 and it leads to a counterexample to the analogue of Theorem 1.2. But unlike in C3 , the
Heisenberg group cousin in A3 is not sticky. It appears likely that in A3 , the sticky case of (Wolff
axiom) Kakeya conjecture is true, but the general conjecture is false.
   As of a couple years ago, I was quite pessimistic about reducing the general case of Kakeya to
the sticky case.
6                                                     LARRY GUTH


   The first indication that the sticky case may play a crucial role in problems of this type was
the solution of the Furstenberg set conjecture by Orponen-Shmerkin and Ren-Wang in 2024. The
Furstenberg set conjecture is another important problem in geometric measure theory. In the
late 90s, Tom Wolff identified the Kakeya problem, the Falconer problem, and the Furstenberg
set problem as cousin problems involving related issues. In 2024, in [12], Orponen and Shmerkin
proved the sticky case of the Furstenberg set conjecture. Shortly afterwards, in [14], Ren and Wang
proved the full Furstenberg conjecture. Their proof reduces the general problem to the sticky case
and a case which is far from sticky, which they call the semi-well-spaced case. They resolve the
semi-well-spaced case using Fourier analytic methods building on [6]. And they give a short and
elegant multiscale argument which reduces the general Furstenberg conjecture to these two cases.
   It was quite surprising to me that the general Furstenberg problem reduces to these two cases.
This work gave a hint that the sticky case might be a key case in other problems as well.

   In 2025, Wang and Zahl did indeed reduce the general case of the Kakeya problem to the sticky
case.

                            6. New ideas for the not sticky case
   We now begin to discuss the paper [18], which reduces the general case of the Kakeya conjecture
to the sticky case. We will describe a small variation of the argument from [18], which hopefully
simplifies some minor details. This part of our survey will be more detailed than before, but the
writeup does not include all details and is not completely precise. Our goal is to describe the new
ideas in the proof.
   Define β to be the infimal number so that, whenever ∆max (T) ⪅ 1, we have


(3)                                         µ(T) ⪅ |T|β
   Now suppose that T is arranged in a worst case way, so ∆max (T) ⪅ 1 and µ(T) ≈ |T|β . Without
loss of generality, we can assume that the tubes of T lie in the unit ball B1 . Because ∆max (T) ⪅ 1,
we know that ∆(T, B1 ) ⪅ 1 and so |T| ⪅ δ −2 . For these notes, we focus on the case that |T| ≈ δ −2 .
This case involves most of the ideas in the general proof and is technically simpler.
   Our goal is to prove that the worst case is sticky. Assuming that β > 0 and that T is not sticky,
we will prove that µ(T) ≪ |T|β . (We use the notation µ(T) ≪ |T|β to mean that µ(T) is much less
than |T|β ). This contradicts our assumption that µ(T) ≈ |T|β , and so we conclude that T must be
sticky. But then the sticky Kakeya theorem implies that β = 0.
   In order to use the definition of β, we need to relate T with other sets of tubes. We relate T to
some other set of tubes T′ with ∆max (T′ ) ⪅ 1 and we use that µ(T′ ) ⪅ |T′ |β . Over the course of
the proof, we will bring into play several other sets of tubes T′ . One key point is to see which sets
of tubes T′ we can bring into play.
   Since |T| ∼ δ −2 , if T is not sticky it means that there is some scale ρ ∈ [δ, 1] so that |TTρ | ≪
(ρ/δ)−2 and so |Tρ | ≫ ρ−2 . We will assume that T is not-sticky-at-all-scales, meaning that for
every ρ in the range δ ≪ ρ ≪ 1, we have

                                 |TTρ | ≪ (ρ/δ)−2 and |Tρ | ≫ ρ−2 .
At the very end, we will discuss how to reduce the not-sticky case to the not-sticky-at-all-scales
case.

has a very delicate structure that causes tubes to overlap a lot, and I don’t see any way of sliding
the tubes around without breaking the structure and decreasing µ(T).

6.3. A surprising induction. This is a key philosophical moment in the proof. We are going to
try to control TB using induction. But the set of tubes TB need not obey ∆max (TB ) ⪅ 1. This
makes it surprising to try to use TB in an inductive proof.
   To put this moment in context, let us recall some background about the multilinear Kakeya
problem. The multilinear Kakeya problem is a cousin of the Kakeya probelm that was solved by
Bennett-Carbery-Tao in [4]. The proof was simplified in [5], and the proof there is only a few pages
long. Multilinear Kakeya involves n sets of tubes Tj in Rn , where the tubes of Tj are approximately
parallel to the xj axis. Multilinear Kakeya is important because it is much easier than Kakeya but
still has many applications - for instance in work of Bourgain-Demeter on decoupling theory [3].
   The key feature that makes multilinear Kakeya much easier than Kakeya is that if we intersect
the tubes of each Tj with a small ball B, then the resulting sets of tubes Tj,B obey the hypotheses
of multilinear Kakeya. Therefore, we can easily apply induction to study the intersections of tubes
in each small ball B. In contrast, the hypotheses of the Kakeya probem do not behave well when
we restrict the tubes of T to a small ball B.
   Nevertheless, Wang and Zahl were able to use TB in an inductive argument. While TB itself
does not obey ∆max (TB ) ⪅ 1, Wang and Zahl manage to relate TB to some other sets of tubes T̃
with ∆max (T̃) ⪅ 1, and then we can use that µ(T̃) ⪅ |T̃|β . We will explore how they carry out this
induction.

  So far, we have discussed the special case ∆max (TB ) ⪅ 1. Now we have to analyze the case
∆max (TB ) ≫ 1.
  If ∆max (TB ) ≫ 1, it means that for some convex set K ⊂ B, ∆(TB , K) ≫ 1. Wang and Zahl
consider the set K that maximizes ∆(TB , K). The argument depends on the shape of K. We will

6.6. How do we reduce to the not-sticky-at-all-scales case? We saw in the argument above
that it was important to reduce to the not-sticky-at-all-scales case. In this subsection, we explain
how to do that. We start by recalling the sticky case, the not-sticky case, and the not-sticky-at-all-
scales case.
   Recall that T is a set of δ-tubes in B1 with ∆max (T) ⪅ 1 and that |T| ≈ δ −2 .
                    INTRODUCTION TO THE PROOF OF THE KAKEYA CONJECTURE                                  15


    The sticky case means that for every ρ ∈ [δ, 1], ∆max (Tρ ) ⪅ 1. Since |T| ≈ δ −2 , this is equivalent
to |TTρ | ≈ (δ/ρ)−2 .
    If T is not sticky, it means that there is some ρ ∈ [δ, 1] so that |TTρ | ≪ (δ/ρ)−2 . Such a ρ must
lie in the range δ ≪ ρ ≪ 1.
    We say that T is not-sticky-at-all-scales if |TTρ | ≪ (δ/ρ)−2 for every ρ in the range δ ≪ ρ ≪ 1.
    Here is the rough idea how to reduce the not-sticky case to the not-sticky-at-all-scales case.
Suppose that there is some scale ρ so that |TTρ | ≈ (δ/ρ)−2 . It follows that ∆max (Tρ ) ⪅ 1. Now we
can bound

                                         µ(T) ⪅ µ(TTρ )µ(Tρ ),
which reduces our original problem to two similar problems at smaller scales. We try to keep
reducing in this way. If one of the smaller problems is not-sticky-at-all-scales then we are stuck and
we cannot reduce further. Otherwise we can reduce further. If can keep reducing this way to very
small problems, it means that our original set of tubes T was sticky, and we can handle it using the
sticky Kakeya theorem. Otherwise, we get stuck with a problem that is not-sticky-at-all-scales and
we can handle it using the argument we have sketched in this section.
   (This reduction requires some care with small parameters to do in a rigorous way.)

we estimate µ(T′ ) using the high density lemma. When we look inside the proof of the high density
lemma, we examine T̃′ at various scales. We eventually find some sets of tubes T̃ with ∆max (T̃) ⪅ 1.
We can apply our inductive assumption to each such T̃. In this subtle way, we were able to study
TB using induction.

in the ring AN can be written as r = j=0 aj xj with aj ∈ Fp . If j(r) is the least j so that aj ̸= 0,
then ∥r∥AN = p−j . I think that if N is sufficiently large, then the argument reducing Kakeya to
sticky Kakeya has a good chance to work in the ring AN .
   More broadly, we can ask: “For which problems of Kakeya type is the worst case sticky?” From
the Katz-Zahl example, we learn that we should consider problems with many different scales. As
far as I know, it looks plausible that for a broad range of Kakeya type problems involving many
scales, the worst case is sticky. On the other hand, in the Furstenberg set problem, the known
worst case example comes from a grid, and it is not sticky. It looks plausible that examples which
are sticky in a strong sense cannot match the worse case in the Furstenberg set problem. It would
be interesting to explore how much the proof from [18] can be generalized.

                              9. What about higher dimensions?
   It is natural to ask about generalizing this work to higher dimensions. No one knows yet how
to do that. There is one key difference in dimension n ≥ 4. Our whole discussion has been based
around the convex Wolff axioms. But it is well known that in dimension at least 4, the analogue of
the Kakeya conjecture with convex Wolff axioms is not true. Let us write down a precise question
and then recall the example.
   Our definitions of density and ∆max make sense in any dimension.
   Suppose that T is a set of δ tubes in Rn . For any convex set K ⊂ Rn , we define the density the
density of T in K as

   In dimension 4, there is an example of a set of δ-tubes T with ∆max (T) ≲ 1 and µ(T) = δ −1 . In
this example, the tubes are all contained in the δ-neighborhood of a degree 2 algebraic hypersurface,
such as

                                 Z = {x ∈ R4 : x21 + x22 − x23 − x24 = 1}
A typical point of Z lies in a 1-parameter family of lines in Z. For instance, if we intersect Z with
the hyperplane x1 = 1, then we get a 2-dimensional cone defined by x22 − x23 − x24 = 0, and this cone
contains a 1-parameter family of lines. So the point (1, 0, 0, 0) lies in a 1-parameter family of lines
in Z. But the variety Z is very symmetric. The Lie group O(2, 2) acts transitively on Z. Since
this is a linear action, the action maps lines to lines. And so every point of Z lies in a 1-parameter
family of lines in Z.
   The tubes of T are δ-neighborhoods of these lines. There are δ −3 tubes of T. The set U (T) is
the δ-neighborhood of Z, and so |U (T)| ∼ δ and so µ(T) ∼ δ −1 .
   The tubes of T cluster near the algebraic surface Z, but they don’t cluster in any convex set. It
is not difficult to check that for any convex set K, ∆(T, K) ≲ 1, and so ∆max (T) ≲ 1.
   There are two possible ways around this example. One way is to focus on the original version of
the Kakeya conjecture with direction-separated tubes. In this setup, we suppose that T is a set of
δ −(n−1) δ-tubes in Rn in δ-separated directions. (The example above is not a counterexample to this
direction separated Kakeya conjecture, because the directions of the tubes in the example all lie near
a degree 2 algebraic surface in S 3 . ) Working with convex Wolff axioms played an important role
in the reduction to the sticky case. Can it be made to work in the directon-separated framework?
   The second way around this example is to replace the convex Wolff axioms with more general
polynomial Wolff axioms that take account of how tubes of T pack into neighborhoods of low degree
algebraic varieties. I think this is a natural direction to pursue. But replacing convex sets by semi-
algebraic sets is a significant issue and is not just a technical modification of the 3-dimensional
proof.
