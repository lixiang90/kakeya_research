1.Introduction
In this paper, we prove lower bounds on the discretized incidences between
hyperplanes and points inRd. For any dyadic scaleδ >0, aδ-hyperplane
is the dual set of the points covered by a dyadic cube. We are interested in
the following problem. LetPbe a finite set ofδ-cubes inRd, and letHbe a
finite set ofδ-hyperplanes. Suppose that, for each cubeQinP, the number
ofH∈ Hwhich intersectQis at leastM. How large mustHbe?
To be nontrivial, we need additional assumptions about how ‘separated’
theδ-hyperplanes are. We will use a Frostman-type non-concentration con-
dition to prove our main theorem. This non-concentration condition is the
natural analog of the non-concentration condition used by Ren and Wang
[34] in their solution of the Furstenberg problem in the plane. We defer the
precise definitions to Section 2.1.

2.1.Discretized incidences.We will writeA≲Bto denoteA≤cB, for

Letδbe a dyadic scale, i.e.,δ= 2 −n for somen∈N. For any setA⊂R d,
we denote byD δ(A)the set of half-open, half-closed dyadic cubes of side
lengthδthat intersectA. We will also use the notationD δ :=D δ(Rd).
We call a set of hyperplanes aδ-hyperplane if it is the dual set of the
points covered by a cubeQ∈ D δ. We denote the set ofδ-hyperplanes by
Hδ. We say that aδ-cubeQintersects aδ-hyperplaneH, ifHcontains a
hyperplane that meetsQ, and we denote this byH∩Q̸=∅. In the special
case whend= 2, we callδ-hyperplanesδ-tubes.
In [34] Ren and Wang proved sharp bounds on the incidences between
δ-tubes andδ-cubes inR 2, leading to a resolution of the Furstenberg set
problem in the plane. They introduced the following definition, and proved
Theorem 2.2 below (see Theorem 4.1 in [34]).
Lets∈[0, d]andc >0. A non-empty bounded setA⊆R d is called a
(δ, s, c)-set if it satisfies the following Frostman-type condition. For every
integer0≤m≤nand eachQ∈ D 2−m,D δ(A∩Q)contains at most ac2 −sm
portion of all the cubes inDδ(A).
Remark 2.1.There is an alternative definition of(δ, s, c)-sets in the lit-
erature. For example, in Ren and Wang’s solution to the Furstenberg set
problem [34],Ais called a(δ, s, c)-set if for every ballBof radiusr∈[δ,1],
Dδ(A∩B)contains at most acr s portion of all the cubes inDδ(A). It is
immediate that the two definitions are equivalent in the sense that, ifAis a
(δ, s, c)-set under one definition, then it is a(δ, s, c′)-set in the other, where
c′ andcagree up to a multiplicative absolute constant that depends only on
the ambient dimensiond.
WhenP ⊆ D δ, we say thatPis a(δ, s, c)-set if the set of points inR d
covered by the cubes inPis a(δ, s, c)-set. A setHofδ-hyperplanes inR d
is called a(δ, s, c)-set if its dual is a(δ, s, c)-set.

4.1.Discretized version.We will prove Theorem 1.1 by induction on the
dimension of the spaceRd, with Theorem 2.2 as a base case. Our inductive

We say that a pair(P,H)⊂ Dδ([0,1] d)× Hδ is a(δ, s, δ−η, M)-nice config-
uration, if for eachQ∈Pthere are∼Mmanyδ-hyperplanes inHthrough
Q, with the property that the set of directions of theseδ-hyperplanes is a
(δ, s, δ−η)-set. (More precisely, for eachH⊥ we fix aδ-cube that meetsH⊥,

inHsuch that theδ-cubes of the normal vectors of theseδ-hyperplanes are
distinct and their union is a(δ, s, δ−η)-set ofδ-cubes.)
We often find it useful to identify the set of directions inRd, i.e. the sphere
Sd−1, byR d−1, via radial projections. This can be done, say, by fixing an
affine hyperplaneHat distance 1 from the origin, and then identifying each
direction by the point at which the line through the origin of this direction
hits the planeH. We will refer to this mapping as the ’radial projection
ontoH’.
A technical issue that we need to be careful about is that not every line
meetsH(those whose direction is parallel toHdon’t), and moreover, al-
though this mapping from the sphere toHis locally bi-Lipschitz, and there-
fore it preserves many nice properties, unfortunately it gets distorted as the
directions of the lines are getting close to being parallel toH. Therefore, it is
nottrue that the radial image of a(δ, s, δ−η)-set of directions is a(δ, s, δ−η)-
set onH.
However, by throwing away a constant portion of our(δ, s, δ−η)-set of
directions, we can find a planeHsuch that each remaining direction has at
least a constant angle with this planeHand therefore our mapping will be
bi-Lipschitz with an absolute constant. Noting also that a constant portion
of a(δ, s, δ −η)-set is a(δ, s, cδ −η)-set, this argument shows that for each
(δ, s, δ−η)-set of directions there is a planeHsuch that the radial projection
onto this planeHcontains a(δ, s, cδ−η)-set of comparable size. Moreover, we
can find not only oneHwith this property but this will be true for a constant
portion of all affine hyperplanesH(among those whose distance from the
origin is 1). Every constant in this observation is an absolute constant that
depends only on the dimensiond.
