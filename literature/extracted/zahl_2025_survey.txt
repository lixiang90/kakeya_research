occurred after the excellent 2002 survey paper [40]. In particular, we will not discuss the techniques and results
of Davies [15] and Cordoba [14], who proved Conjectures 1.1 and 1.3 in R2 . Nor will we discuss the geometric

     2.1 Near misses to the Kakeya conjecture in R3 : the Heisenberg group and SL2 examples. A
near miss to the Kakeya set conjecture is a set that has properties in common with a Besicovitch set, but which
has dimension (or some suitable analogue, such as cardinality) smaller than what is predicted by the Kakeya
set conjecture. A near miss to the Kakeya set conjecture helps us understand the limitations of certain proof
techniques—if a proof technique cannot distinguish between a genuine Kakeya set in Rn and a near-miss, then
this proof technique cannot prove the Kakeya set conjecture. A near miss to the Kakeya set conjecture thus forms
a barrier to proving the Kakeya set conjecture. This is (loosely) analogous to the relativization, natural proofs,
and algebrization barriers from complexity theory.

group example, on the other hand, point in a positive codimension set of directions in C3 .

upper Minkowski dimension 5/2 + c0 .

    As discussed above, Katz, Łaba, and Tao’s proof of Theorem 2.3 used the fact that a Besicovitch set in
R3 contains a line pointing in every direction—this is a property that does not hold for the Heisenberg group
example. To make use of this property, Katz, Łaba, and Tao used the following “sums versus differences” result
from additive combinatorics:
   Theorem 2.4. Let G be an Abelian group, and let A, B ⊂ G with #A ≤ N, #B ≤ N, and #(A + B) ≤ N .
Then #(A − B) ≲ N 2−c , where c > 0 is an absolute constant.
A weaker form of Theorem 2.4 with o(N 2 ) in place of N 2−c was first proved by Balog and Szemerédi [2], and the
version stated above follows from the work of Gowers [22]. Theorem 2.4 as stated was proved and first used in
the context of the Kakeya problem by Bourgain [6].
    The idea is to apply Theorem  S 2.4 to three slices of the Besicovitch set—the sets A, B, and (A + B)/2
correspond to the intersection of T T with the planes {x = 0}, {x = 1}, and {x = 1/2} respectively, while A − B
represents the set of directions of the tubes in T. However, a naive application of this method only establishes
that Besicovitch sets in R3 have dimension at least 2 + c for a small constant c > 0.
    To prove Theorem 2.3, Katz, Łaba and Tao analyzed the structure of a (hypothetical) Besicovitch set in R3
with Minkowski dimension exactly 5/2. If such a Besicovitch set is discretized at scale δ, it yields a set T of δ tubes
pointing in δ-separated directions with the following property: #T ∼ δ −2 , and for both ρ = δ 1/2 and ρ = δ 1/4 ,
there exists a set Tρ of ρ tubes pointing in ρ-separated directions with the property that each T ∈ T is contained
in a ρ tube, and each ρ tube contains about (ρ/δ)2 tubes from T. This motivates the following definition. In
what follows, we say that a set of ρ tubes Tρ (or more generally, a collection of convex sets) is a cover 1 of T if
each tube in T is contained in at least one, and at most O(1) tubes from Tρ , and each tube from Tρ contains
approximately the same number of tubes from T.
    Definition 2.5. Let T be a set of δ tubes in Rn of cardinality #T ∼ δ 1−n . We say that T is sticky if for all
δ ≤ ρ ≤ 1, there exists a cover Tρ of T that satisfies the Convex Wolff Axioms.
Stickiness was first introduced in [36], though our definition above is motivated by more recent results and is
slightly anachronistic.

     Katz and the author’s proof of Theorem 2.6 loosely parallels an analogous proof in the finite field setting due
to Bourgain, Katz, and Tao [10] (this latter proof uses a finite field analogue of the Erdős-Szemerédi sum-product
theorem [20] in place of Theorem 2.7). In brief, the proof is as follows. Suppose for contradiction that there is a
set T of δ satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem 2.7 for which (2.6) fails. After pigeonholing it is possible to find
pairs of tubes T1 , T2 and TA , TB and a set T1,2 ⊂ T (resp. TA,B ⊂ T) of cardinality roughly δ −1 that intersect
both T1 and T2 (resp. TA and TB ). Each tube in T1,2 intersects roughly δ −1/2 tubes from TA,B , and vice-versa.
The situation thus far is consistent with both the Heisenberg group and SL2 examples. If one applies these
arguments to the Heisenberg group example, then for a typical tube T ∈ T1,2 , the points of intersection from
tubes in T ′ ∈ TA,B , i.e. the set {T ′ ∩ T : T ′ ∈ TA.B } are spread-out along T . In fact they obey a Frostman-type
non-concentration condition. At the opposite extreme, if one applies these arguments to the SL2 example, then
the set {T ′ ∩ T : T ′ ∈ TA.B } is concentrated into a short tube segment (the analogue of a tube segment of length
δ 1/2 ).
      Guided by the behavior of these two examples, Katz and the author showed that every counter-example to
Theorem 2.6 must be either of “Heisenberg type” or “SL2 type.” Different arguments are used to handle these
two cases.
      If T is of Heisenberg type, then we can identify each tube T ∈ TA,B with a δ ball in R2 , and we can identify
the tubes in T1,2 with δ intervals in [0, 1] (this latter identification is not one-to-one), so that the corresponding
union of points and intervals violates Theorem 2.7. This is impossible, and we conclude that the original set T
must have satisfied (2.6).
      If T is of SL2 type, then elementary geometric arguments are used to show that the tubes in T must cluster
into twisting planks called regulus strips, and these regulus strips must intersect in a carefully prescribed manner
that violates (a curved variant of) the Szemerédi-Trotter theorem. This is impossible, and we (again) conclude
that the original set T must have satisfied (2.6).
      While the ideas used to prove Theorem 2.6 did not play a substantial role in the resolution of Conjecture 1.1
in R3 , the analysis of unions of regulus strips continues to be an area of active study that is closely related to
questions in projection theory in Euclidean space and the first Heisenberg group.

     3.1 The sticky case. In the early 2000s, Katz and Tao formulated a program for proving the Kakeya
set conjecture in R3 . They hypothesized that every counter-example to Conjecture 1.2(A) in R3 must have the
structural properties stickiness, planiness, and graininess described in Section 2.2. They then formulated a plan
to leverage these properties to show that a counter-example to Conjecture 1.2(A) would imply the existence of
a set that violates Bourgain’s discretized sum-product theorem [7] which was a precursor to (and close relative
of) Theorem 2.7. In a talk and accompanying blog post [48], Tao explained the Katz-Tao program for solving
the Kakeya set conjecture in R3 . At the time, it was not clear whether every (hypothetical) counter-example to
Conjecture 1.2(A) must be sticky. In particular, the discovery of the SL2 example suggested that in order to
prove the Kakeya set conjecture in R3 , it would be necessary to study sets that are far from being sticky.

argument, it gives an improved Kakeya maximal function bound for n ≥ 7; it also gives new Hausdorff dimension
bounds in certain dimensions n ≥ 7 (for certain other dimensions n ≥ 7 and for Minkowski dimension bounds,
the best-known estimate comes from arguments based on Theorem 2.4; see [39] for details).
     The proof of Theorem 4.4 uses a technique developed by Guth [27] in the context of the restriction conjecture.
The idea is as follows. First, we consider the case k =S n − 1. Let T be a set of δ tubes. Using the Guth-Katz
polynomial partitioning technique from [31], we cover T T by a union of semi-algebraic grains—a semi-algebraic
grain is a set of the form S = B(x, r) ∩ Nδ (Z), where Z = Z(P ) is the zero-set of a low degree polynomial. This
is called a grains decomposition of T. The Guth-Katz polynomial partitioning technique controls the number of
grains in this decomposition, as well as the number of tubes from T that intersect each grain. We then apply
Theorem 4.3 to the tubes that intersect each grain, and sum the resulting contributions. The estimate becomes
stronger as the number of grains increases. We use the assumption that the tubes satisfy the Polynomial Wolff
Axioms to ensure that the number of grains is suitably large. For k < n − 1, we further decompose each grain in
the above grains decomposition to obtain a multi-level grains decomposition of T. The main new difficulty is to
control the number of tubes that intersect each nested sequence of grains in this multi-level decomposition.

    4.5 Future directions. It is an interesting question what improvements are possible for the broad and
narrow estimates described above, and whether such improvements will play a role in the resolution of the Kakeya
set conjecture in higher dimensions. The Bourgain-Guth multilinear → linear argument was developed in the
context of the Fourier restriction conjecture. The improved broad estimate from Theorem 4.4 was applied to
the Fourier restriction conjecture by Hickman and the author [35] (see also [27, 28, 33] for previous work in this
direction), though this has since been superseded by different methods [23, 49]. It seems plausible that improved
broad and narrow estimates may play a role in future work on the restriction conjecture.
