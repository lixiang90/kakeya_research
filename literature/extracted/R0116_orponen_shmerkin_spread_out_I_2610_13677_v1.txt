                                            1. Introduction

   This paper and its companion [6] concern optimal Furstenberg estimates and restricted projection
theorems for spread-out families of planes. Roughly speaking, a family of planes is spread-out if it is
locally unconcentrated near pencils at most points and scales. The general estimates in [6] are the
main motivation for this work. Here we introduce spread-outness and prove the sticky δ-discretized
Furstenberg estimate, Theorem 1.9, in which the point set is roughly Ahlfors regular (or sticky). The
terminology was introduced in the sticky Kakeya problem [48], where stickiness roughly corresponds
to tubes in nearby directions “sticking together”. Cohen, the second author, Wang and Zakharov
[6] use this estimate to treat general Frostman and Katz–Tao configurations – or, in short, remove
the ”stickiness” hypothesis. The two papers form a single project, presented separately for clarity of
exposition and to distinguish the authorship of the sticky estimate from that of the passage to general
configurations.
   Before stating the δ-discretized result, we record some of its easier-to-state corollaries and appli-
cations. We state the corollaries only in the planar case, although their natural higher-dimensional
counterparts would also follow from Theorem 1.9. The main reason for stating only the planar cases
here is to avoid duplicate work: the higher-dimensional results in full generality will anyway be stated
and proven in [6]. The δ-discretized result in Theorem 1.9 will be stated in all dimensions, since it is
required as a black box in [6].

1.1. Restricted projections. For V ∈ G(d, k), let ΠV : Rd → V denote the orthogonal projection
onto V . The Marstrand-Mattila projection theorem [29, 31] is a cornerstone of geometric measure
theory. It states that if E ⊂ Rd is a Borel set then, denoting Lebesgue measure on the Grassmannian
G(d, k) of k-planes in Rd by γd,k , one has
(1.1)                 dimH ΠV (E) = min{dimH E, k}         for γd,k -a.e. V ∈ G(d, k).
Here dimH is Hausdorff dimension. Projection theory seeks to understand the behavior of dimH ΠV (E)
for ν-typical V , when ν is a highly singular measure on G(d, k), perhaps under additional assumptions
on E. See [47] for a recent survey on the topic, including its connection with the Kakeya problem.
   A natural question is for which measures ν, in place of γd,k , the Marstrand-Mattila estimate (1.1)
continues to hold. This question has been extensively studied in the case of length measure on smooth
curves in G(d, k); see, for example, [16, 24, 39, 19] for G(3, 1), [16, 17] for G(3, 2), and [18, 14, 47] for
higher-dimensional Grassmannians and further references. By now, the picture in this case is fairly
complete in dimension and co-dimension 1 (k = 1 or d − 1), but much less is known for 1 < k < d − 1.

All the proofs in this line of work rely on the smoothness of the curve, and it is not clear how to
extend them to more general measures. In a different direction, Chen [5] proved a restricted projection
theorem for a class of random measures on G(3, 1); the argument does not extend to G(2, 1).
    In particular, up to now there were no genuine restricted Marstrand-type results for measures on
G(2, 1). The following Theorem 1.1 is a consequence of our main result. We write dimP for packing
dimension. Recall that a locally finite Borel measure µ on a metric space X is called uniformly perfect
if diam(spt µ) > 0, and there exist β ∈ [0, 1) and D > 1 such that
                                     µ(B(x, r)) ≤ β · µ(B(x, Dr))
for all closed balls B(x, r) ⊂ X such that spt µ ̸⊂ B(x, Dr). In particular, it is easy to see that
every non-trivial Ahlfors s-regular measure with s > 0 is uniformly perfect. (A measure µ on a
metric space X is Ahlfors s-regular if there is C ≥ 1 such that C −1 rs ≤ µ(B(x, r)) ≤ Crs for all
x ∈ spt µ, r ∈ (0, diam(spt µ)].)
Theorem 1.1. Let ν be a uniformly perfect measure on G(2, 1). For any Borel set E ⊂ R2 with
dimH (E) = dimP (E),
                      dimH ΠV (E) = min{dimH E, 1}         for ν-a.e. V ∈ G(2, 1).
Remark 1.2. The assumption of equal Hausdorff and packing dimension is not necessary, and it
will be removed in the companion paper [6], where more general higher-dimensional results will be
presented.
   As shown by Kaufman and Mattila [26], there are singular measures ν on G(2, 1), of dimension
arbitrarily close to 1, for which the Marstrand-Mattila estimate fails for some compact set E. Both
the set E and the measure ν in these examples are rather special. In particular, ν is supported on
a“well-spaced” set, that is, a set Θ such that for many pairs of scales 0 < δ ≪ r, Θ ∩ Br is contained
in a δ-ball for all r-balls Br . Uniform perfectness rules out this type of behavior.
   As is usually the case, these results are derived from more general discretized statements, which
are far more useful in applications. The discretized sticky Furstenberg estimate is Theorem 1.9; its
projection counterpart is Theorem 4.1.

1.5. Proof strategy and wider connections. The proof of Theorem 1.9 has several points in
common with previous work by the authors on Furstenberg sets [37, 38].
      (i) We first establish the sticky projection estimate, Theorem 4.1, and then use an argument of
          Hong Wang which first appeared in [38] to deduce the Furstenberg estimate by multiscale
          analysis and pigeonholing.
     (ii) The projection estimate is proved by contradiction. Assuming that the estimate fails by
          a definite amount, we show that, after rescaling, an extremal configuration has a product
          structure inside typical slabs.
    (iii) This product structure contradicts an asymmetric sum-product estimate, Proposition 3.1. Its
          proof uses Balog–Szemerédi–Gowers, Plünnecke–Ruzsa, and an inverse theorem of the second
          author [43, 44].
   Although not used in a direct way, we were also inspired over the years by Hochman’s work on
self-similar sets and measures based on additive combinatorics [22, 23]. At a heuristic level, we see the
results of this work as an extension of Hochman’s ideas to a setting in which the dynamical structure
of self-similar or self-affine measures is replaced by the geometric structure of sticky and spread-out
sets. An important conceptual analogy is that both in Hochman’s work and in the present paper,
                         SPREAD-OUT FURSTENBERG SETS I: THE STICKY CASE                                  7


an inverse theorem for convolutions is used to obtain a small improvement in the dimension of the
object under study, which is then amplified by a multiscale analysis. The mechanisms by which this
is achieved are completely different.
   The use of spread-out sets, introduced in this paper, accounts for several differences from these
earlier arguments. The main properties of spread out objects are:
   (a) Invariance under scaling and refinement (which are immediate from the definitions).
   (b) Invariance under projections; see Lemma 2.6.
   (c) Invariance under localization to small cubes; see Lemma 2.8.
   (d) An L2 -flattening property; see Theorem 3.2.
See §2 for a detailed discussion of spread-outness and its properties, and §6.1 for a proof that uniformly
perfect measures on R (or G(2, 1)) are spread-out.

1.6. Notation. We introduce some notation that will be used throughout the paper.
  Given a finite set P , we write mP for the uniform probability measure on P .
   We write |P | for the cardinality of a finite set P . We use the cube and covering notation of
Section 1.3, extending the same definitions to arbitrary δ > 0. For E ⊂ Rd , its δ-discretization is
E (δ) := zDδ (E) ⊂ δZd , so |E|δ = |E (δ) |. Up to a factor depending only on the ambient dimension,
|E|δ is the minimal number of δ-balls needed to cover E.
   Likewise, the δ-discretization of a finite Borel measure µ on Rd is defined by µ(δ) ({z(Q)}) = µ(Q)
for Q ∈ Dδ (Rd ).
   The open r-neighborhood of a set E ⊂ Rd is denoted by E(r). We write B(0, r) for the ball of
radius r centered at the origin. For λ > 0, λQ and λB denote the concentric dilations of a cube Q
and a ball B by the factor λ.
   If (X, F, µ) is a measure space, and f : X → Y is a map, we write f µ for the push-forward measure
(f µ)(B) := µ(f −1 (B)) on Y .
   We write A ⪅δ B to mean A ≤ B(log(1/δ))C , where the implicit exponent C is independent of δ,
but may depend on the other ambient parameters, which will be specified as needed. We define ⪆δ
similarly.
  By A ≫par 1 we mean that A is sufficiently large, depending on the parameters par. We define
A ≪par 1 similarly.
    We will often work with dyadic scales. Occasionally, we will assume a scale δ to be dyadic even
if it technically does not need to be; this means that δ is replaced by the closest dyadic scale. This
will sometimes cause a constant factor loss, but this is harmless in all cases where we use this slight
abuse of notation.
   We will work with dimensions 1 ≤ k ≤ d. These will be fixed in each section, and we take them to

  Logarithms are always base 2 unless otherwise specified.

Kleinbock, Lindenstrauss and Weiss [28, §2]. Here we test only H ∈ Hd,k and control the mass of
subsets according to their number of singular scales.

                      5. Multiscale and sticky Furstenberg estimates

5.1. Plane and spread-out configurations. A key tool in [38] was the multiscale incidence esti-
mate [37, Proposition 5.2]. Our goal is to derive an analogous estimate for spread-out configurations.
We start by defining the relevant notation.
  For the rest of this section, we fix the dimensions 1 ≤ k < d, and use the notation A ⪅δ B to mean
A ≤ B(log(1/δ))C , where the implicit exponent C may depend on the parameters in Theorem 1.9,
namely d, k, s, t, η. We define ⪆δ and ≈δ similarly.
   It will sometimes be convenient to identify dyadic cubes with their lower-left endpoints, using
the notation z(Q) from Section 1.3. Given a point x ∈ δZD and a dyadic scale ∆ ≥ δ, we write
x(∆) := z(D∆ (x)). We will also silently identify a family of dyadic cubes with its union whenever
there is no risk of confusion.
   Recall the plane configurations (P, Θ) and their Furstenberg sets F(P, Θ) from Definition 1.5. We
emphasize that this definition does not require that zΘ(p) = {θ ∈ Gd,k : Vθ + p ∈ F}. That is, we
allow for “stray” affine planes that pass through p but belong to F by virtue of having the form Vθ + q
for some θ ∈ zΘ(q), with q ̸= p.
   We will be mostly interested in spread-out configurations, as defined in Definition 1.6. Spread-out
configurations are similar to nice configurations introduced in [37, Definition 5.1], and employed in
the statement of the Furstenberg set theorem in [38, Theorem 5.2]. The difference is that we have
replaced Frostman sets in S 1 by spread-out sets in Gd,k .

setting differs from that of [37] in some important ways, we give a full proof of Proposition 5.1 below,
even if it involves some arguments that are very similar to those in [37, Proposition 5.2].

6.2. Applications to discretized sum-product estimates. The discretized sum-product problem
has received considerable attention since the works of Katz and Tao [25], who introduced the problem,
and Bourgain [2], who proved the first nontrivial result:
Theorem 6.4 (Bourgain). For every s ∈ (0, 1) there are ε, κ > 0, depending only on s, such that the
following holds for δ ≪s 1.
  Let A ⊂ [1, 2] be (δ, s, δ −ε )-Frostman. Then
(6.5)                              max{|A + A|δ , |A · A|δ } ≥ δ −(s+κ) .

   It has been an important problem to understand the dependence of κ on s; in Bourgain’s proof
it was tiny and left uncomputed. Quantitative bounds were provided in [20, 30, 40]. As a corollary
of their resolution of the Furstenberg set problem, Ren and Wang [40] showed that one can take
κ = (1/4)s for s ∈ (0, 2/3) (in the case s ∈ (2/3, 1) the sharp upper bound κ = (1 − s)/2 was already
known).
    The problem has also attracted attention in the special case in which A has additional structure,
and in particular in the Ahlfors-regular case. O’Regan [33, Theorem 1.7] proved that, for an s-Ahlfors
regular set A ⊂ [1, 2] with s ∈ (0, 1/2], one has max{|A + A|δ , |A · A|δ } ≥ δ −4s/3+η for every η > 0 and
all sufficiently small δ. As a corollary of Theorem 1.9, we obtain an exponent 3/2 when the dimension
of A is at most 1/2.

6.3. Triple sums for subsets of the parabola. In this section, we write P := {(x, x2 ) : x ∈ [−1, 1]}.
Because the addition map (x, y) 7→ x + y is bi-Lipschitz on disjoint arcs of P (see Lemma 6.10 below),
understanding sums A+B or additive energies E(A, B) for A, B ⊂ P is straightforward. The situation
is much more subtle for triple sums.
   For finite subsets of P, third and higher additive energies have been studied in [4, 32] with a recent
breakthrough achieved in [9]; the corresponding fractal problem was considered in [35, 36], see also
[12, 13] for closely related work. The first author [35, Corollary 1.3] proved that every closed A ⊂ P
with 0 < dimH (A) < 1 satisfies dimH (A+A+A) ≥ 2 dimH (A)+ε(dimH (A)) for some ε(dimH (A)) > 0.
In [36] the result was quantified with ε(dimH A) = min{dimH A/2, 1 − dimH A}. As a particular case
of their results, Algom and the first author [1] established L2 -flattening of Ahlfors-regular sets on the
parabola, which yields additive growth through iterated self-convolutions.
  It follows from [9, Theorem 1.1] and Cauchy–Schwarz that, for every ε > 0 and every finite
Λ ⊂ P, we have |Λ + Λ + Λ| ≳ε |Λ|3−ε . A natural fractal version of this problem is to ask whether
                           SPREAD-OUT FURSTENBERG SETS I: THE STICKY CASE                                         45


dimH (A + A + A) ≥ 3 dimH (A) when dimH (A) is not too large. As an application of the Furstenberg
set estimate in Theorem 1.3, we answer this question in the affirmative when A is Ahlfors regular.

Theorem 6.9. Let s ∈ (0, 1], t ∈ [0, 1]. Let A ⊂ P be a non-empty s-Ahlfors regular set, and let
E ⊂ R2 be a Borel set with dimH E = dimP E = t. Then dimH (A + E) ≥ s + t. In particular, if
s ≤ 1/2, then dimH (A + A + A) = 3s.

   The following is very standard; we include the short argument for completeness.

    Consider the locally bi-Lipschitz invertible map Ψ(x, y) := (x, x2 − y). For z ∈ R2 , the set Ψ(z + P)
