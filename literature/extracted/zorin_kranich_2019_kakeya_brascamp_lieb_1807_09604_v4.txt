1.2. Multilinear Kakeya inequalities. Kakeya–Brascamp–Lieb (KBL) inequal-
ities are generalizations of Brascamp–Lieb inequalities (1.1) in which functions fj ,
constant in directions of the subspaces Tj , are replaced by sums of functions constant
in varying directions. The first KBL inequality was the multilinear Kakeya inequal-
ity proved by Bennett, Carbery, and Tao [BCT06]. Their work was motivated by
applications to the Fourier restriction problem and generalized the Loomis–Whitney
inequality (see Section 1.1.4). A simplified proof of a slightly weaker result, which is
still adequate for all known applications, was given by Guth [Gut15].
   The multilinear Kakeya inequality of Bennett, Carbery, and Tao plays a central
role in the proof of the `2 decoupling theorem for the paraboloid by Bourgain and
Demeter [BD15] (see also [BD17] for a streamlined exposition, in which the multi-
linear Kakeya inequality is applied directly, without passing through the multilinear
restriction theorem). More general KBL inequalities were first proved in [Ben+18,
Theorem 1.2] and applied in the proof of the decoupling theorem for the moment
curve by Bourgain, Demeter, and Guth [BDG16]. We refer to [GZ18] and references
therein for the current state of development in this direction.
   The proofs of the multilinear Kakeya inequality in [BCT06; Gut15] proceed by
induction on scales and do not yield the optimal endpoint result. The endpoint
version of the multilinear Kakeya inequality was established by Guth [Gut10] us-
ing a version of the polynomial method. A more precise result that involves the
transversality parameters (1.10) was obtained in [BG11, Theorem 6]. Guth’s proof
was later simplified by Carbery and Valdimarsson [CV13]. Most recently, Ruixiang
Zhang [Zha18] obtained endpoint KBL inequalities that extend all scale-invariant
BL inequalities (see Section 1.1.3).

1.6.1. Exterior algebra. Let V, W be real vector spaces. Recall that Λk V denotes the
k-th exterior power of V . A bilinear form h·, ·i : V × W → R induces a bilinear form
Λk V × Λk W → R via
(1.20)             hv1 ∧ · · · ∧ vk , w1 ∧ · · · ∧ wk i := det(hvi , wj i)ki,j=1 .
In particular, an inner product on V induces an inner product on Λk V , which in
turn induces a norm.
  Throughout this article n will remain fixed. We write Λk := Λk Rn and let
                     |Λk | := {v1 ∧ · · · ∧ vk | v1 , . . . , vk ∈ Rn }/{±1}
denote the set of simple k-fold wedge products of vectors in Rn modulo multiplication
by ±1. We work with |Λk | in place of Λk in order to avoid dependence on choices of
orientation of various spaces.
   The wedge product ∧ is well-defined as a map |Λk1 | × |Λk2 | → |Λk1 +k2 |, the Hodge
star ? is well-defined as a map from |Λk | to |Λn−k |, and the absolute value of the inner
product (induced by (1.20) from the Euclidean inner product) is well-defined as a
map from |Λk |×|Λk | to [0, ∞). In particular, also the norm |v| is well-defined on |Λk |.
More geometrically, |v1 ∧ · · · ∧ vk | is the k-dimensional volume of the parallelepiped
spanned by v1 , . . . , vk .
   These operations can be extended to measures as follows. Given measures µ1 , µ2 on
|Λk1 | and |Λk2 |, we denote by µ1 ∧µ2 the measure on |Λk1 +k2 | that is the pushforward
of µ1 × µ2 under ∧. Similarly, given a measure µ on |Λ1 |, we denote by µ∧k the
measure on |Λk | that is the pushforward of the k-fold product µ × · · · × µ under the
map (v1 , . . . , vk ) 7→ v1 ∧ · · · ∧ vk . Given a measure µ on |Λk |, we denote by ?µ the
measure on |Λn−k | that is the pushforward of µ under ?. Given measures µ1 , µ2 on
|Λk |, we denote by hµ1 , µ2 i the measure on [0, ∞) that is the pushforward of µ1 × µ2
under the map (v1 , v´2 ) 7→ |hv1 , v2 i|. Finally, we denote the first moment of a measure
µ on |Λk | by |µ| := |v| dµ(v).

2.2. The Borsuk–Ulam theorem and a covering lemma. The existence of a
polynomial p claimed in Theorem 2.2 can be equivalently stated by saying that the

Theorem 2.4 (Borsuk–Ulam). Let N ≥ J and suppose that F : SN → RJ is a
continuous function that satisfies F (−x) = −F (x) for all x ∈ SN . Then there exists
an x ∈ SN such that F (x) = 0.
  See [Mat03] for a discussion of applications and various equivalent forms of this
theorem. Some of these equivalent forms (known as Lusternik–Schnirelmann results)
take the form of covering statements for the sphere. We will use one such statement.
Lemma 2.5 ([CV13, §5]). Suppose that Ai ⊆ SN for 1 ≤ i ≤ J, and suppose that
for each i, Ai ∩ (−Ai ) = ∅. If J ≤ N , then the 2J sets Ai and −Ai do not cover SN .
  Note that no topological hypothesis on the sets Ai is needed.
Proof. Without loss of generality we may assume that all sets Ai are non-empty.
Define F : SN → RJ by Fi (x) := d(x, −Ai )−d(x, Ai ) for 1 ≤ i ≤ J and x ∈ SN . Then
F is continuous and F (−x) = −F (x) for all x, so by Theorem 2.4 there is an x ∈ SN
with F (x) = F (−x) = 0. We claim that this x does not belong to any Ai or to −Ai .
Indeed, if x ∈ Ai , then d(x, Ai ) = 0, hence also d(x, −Ai ) = Fi (x) + d(x, Ai ) = 0,
hence x ∈ −Ai , a contradiction. If x ∈ −Ai , then we obtain a contradiction by the
same argument with x replaced by −x.                                                 
