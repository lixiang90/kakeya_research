BRENDAN MURPHY AND JONATHAN PAKIANATHAN
Abstract. In this paper, we study continuous Kakeya line and needle con ﬁg-
urations, of both the oriented and unoriented varieties, in connected Lie groups
and some associated homogenous spaces. These are the analog s of Kakeya line
(needle) sets (subsets of Rn where it is possible to turn a line (respectively
an interval of unit length) through all directions continuously , without re-
peating a ”direction” .) W e show under some general assumptions that any
such continuous Kakeya line conﬁguration set in a connected Lie group must
contain an open neighborhood of the identity, and hence must have positive
Haar measure. In connected nilpotent Lie groups G, the only subspace of G
that contains such an unoriented line conﬁguration is shown to be G itself.
Finally some similar questions in homogeneous spaces are ad dressed.
These questions were motivated by work of Z. Dvir in the ﬁnite ﬁeld setting.
Keywords: Kakeya needle sets, Lie groups, homogeneous spaces, ﬁber bu n-
dles.

1. Introduction
The purpose of this note is to study Kakeya like conﬁgurations in the setting of
connected Lie groups. We will only be looking at continuous conﬁgura tions and so
this paper has little to say about the famous Kakeya conjecture (d escribed below)
but instead focuses on Kakeya needle-like conﬁgurations in genera l settings.

around continuously while remaining entirely in the subset. Such a subset is
called a Kakeya needle set. When the subset is required to be convex , Pal ([Pa])
showed the answer was given by the equilateral triangle of base heig ht 1 (area

Besicovitch also considered more general Kakeya sets which were d eﬁned as sub-

Regardless these examples have maximal Hausdorﬀ dimension n and the famous
Kakeya conjecture asks to show that this is true in general, i.e., tha t every Kakeya
subset of Rn has Hausdorﬀ (and hence Minkowski) dimension n. This conjecture
is still open for n ≥ 3. This conjecture is related to many questions in Harmonic
analysis, for example C. Feﬀerman ([Fe]) used Besicovitch’s constru ction of Kakeya
sets of measure zero to disprove the ball multiplier conjecture for Lp(Rn), p ̸= 2.
Further interesting applications to analysis are discussed in [Bo], [Ta ] and [Wo].
In this paper we will not consider Kakeya sets but only conﬁguration s similar to
the Kakeya needle sets where the needle is required to move continu ously. In fact,
we will most often be interested in continuous line conﬁgurations whe re the motion
goes thru a line in each direction (or parallel class) exactly once. The se are sets
in which it is possible to move a line continuously such that every directio n in the
ambient space is achieved exactly once.
More precisely, we will consider continuous functions
σ : RP n−1 → Rn
where RP n−1 is the projective space of Rn, i.e., the space of lines through the origin
in Rn.
For each line L through the origin, σ picks out a displacement σ (L) in a con-
tinuous manner and we study the set |σ | = ∪ L∈RP n−1(σ (L) + L). Notice this set
contains a line in every direction but is more constrained as the line plac ement
varies continuously and since the conﬁguration is minimal in the sense that for
each line through the origin, exactly one line parallel to it is used during the
motion. We call such a σ , a continuous unoriented Kakeya line conﬁguration and
|σ | is the underlying space of the motion.
Similarly we consider continuous functions
σ : Sn−1 → Rn
and call them continuous oriented Kakeya line conﬁgurations.
Notice for either the oriented or unoriented conﬁgurations above , one can con-
sider either the space |σ | swept out of the lines of the conﬁguration or the smaller
needle space |ˆσ | = ∪ L∈RP n−1(σ (L) + I(L)) where I(L) is the interval of length 1
centered about the origin in L. The classical Kakeya conjecture is equivalent to
showing for any σ : RP n−1 → R (not necessarily continuous or even Borel measur-
able), that any Borel set containing |ˆσ | has Hausdorﬀ dimension n. In this paper
we will not address this and consider exclusively only continuous σ .
This study was motivated by a paper of Dvir ([Dv]) which looked at an an alogous
question over ﬁnite ﬁelds. Dvir showed that if E is a subset of a ﬁnite dimensional
vector space over a ﬁnite ﬁeld which contains a line in every direction, then E

We deﬁne an analog of this question in any connected Lie group and in f act any

the basic questions about these Kakeya conﬁgurations. Here the projective space

(Though one could generalize even further to Riemannian manifolds a nd geodesics,
to get a single controlling ”direction” space, one would have to assum e a very
controlled parallelization of the tangent bundle and we do not pursue this here.)
The techniques are mostly from algebraic topology and though some more can
be said using measure theory and harmonic analysis, we will not do so in this note,
choosing to focus primarily on the topological aspects. As a course , we will not shy
away from using some basic machinery, even though a more elementa ry proof might
be available, if we feel the general viewpoint sheds more light on the s ituation in
other spaces.
The underlying space of the conﬁguration σ : RP n−1 → Rn is deﬁned to be
|σ |= ∪ L∈RP n−1(σ (L) + L). A continuous unoriented Kakeya line set is a subset of
Rn that contains the underlying space of some continuous unoriented Kakeya line
conﬁguration. Intuitively, it is a set where it is possible to move a line co ntinuously
such that during the motion every parallel class of lines is used exact ly once.
Using some basic algebraic topology, we show:
Theorem 1.1. The only continuous unoriented Kakeya line set in Rn is Rn itself,
i.e., for every continuous map σ : RP n−1 → Rn, |σ |= ∪ L∈RPn−1(σ (L) + L) = Rn.
Examples are also given showing that this theorem fails if either the co ntinuity
assumption is dropped or if more than one line from each parallel class is allowed
during the motion (i.e., if σ assigns more than one output to an input and is not a
function).
In the oriented case we show:
Theorem 1.2. The only continuous oriented Kakeya line set in R2n+1 is R2n+1
itself, i.e., for every continuous map σ : S2n → R2n+1, |σ | = ∪ x∈S2n (σ (x) +
Lx) = R2n+1 for any n ≥ 1. Here Lx is the line passing thru the origin and x
oriented in direction pointing from the origin to x. On the other hand, in R2n there
exist continuous oriented Kakeya line sets |σ | that are disjoint from any prescribed
bounded set, and so in particular |σ | need not be all of R2n.
Again this theorem fails if either σ is not continuous or if σ is not a function,
i.e., more than one line in a given direction is used during the motion.
It is now not hard to extend these questions to the setting of conn ected Lie
groups G. Such a Lie group has a Lie algebra g, and it is well known (see Warner)
that the Lie subalgebras of g are in one to one correspondence with the connected
subgroups of G. In particular for every line through the origin in g there corresponds
a unique one-parameter subgroup of G (which will either be an immersed line or
a circle group.) through the exponential-log correspondence. The one-parameter
subgroups of Rn are exactly the lines through the origin and these will take the
place of lines in a general connected Lie group. The analog of parallel lines, will be
left translates gH of a given one parameter subgroup H. (One can also replace left

for a given ﬁnite dimensional, real, Lie algebra g it is often suﬃcient to understand
the questions about continuous Kakeya line conﬁgurations in the un ique simply-
connected Lie group with Lie algebra g.

2. Continuous Kakeya Line Configurations in Rn
Deﬁnition 2.1. A continuous unoriented Kakeya line conﬁguration in Rn is a
continuous map σ : RP n−1 → Rn. The underlying space of σ , |σ | is deﬁned as
|σ |= ∪ L∈RP n−1(σ (L) + L)
The associated unoriented Kakeya needle set is |ˆσ |= ∪ L∈RP n−1(σ (L) +I(L)) where
I(L) is the interval of length 1 centered around the origin in L. For 1 ≤ R < ∞ , the
R-elongation of the Kakeya needle set is the set obtained when we replace I(L) with
an interval of length R centered abound the origin in L in this deﬁnition. Similar
deﬁnitions hold for oriented variants where RP n−1 is replaced by Sn−1.
Note since projective spaces, spheres and intervals are compact spaces, it is not
hard to show that the associated Kakeya needle sets (oriented or unoriented) and
their elongations are compact subspaces of Rn when σ is continuous. Thus |σ |itself
is σ -compact and hence a Borel set.
Consider the trivial vector bundle ǫn given by π : RP n−1 × Rn → RP n−1.

w(ξ) = 1 + w1(ξ) + w2(ξ) + . . . . If ξ = ξ1 ⊕ ξ2 then w(ξ) = w(ξ1)w(ξ2) by Cartan’s
formula. Finally recall w(ǫk) = 1 for any trivial bundle ǫk and w(γ) = 1 + a where
a is a generator of H 1(RP n−1, F2).

formula that
1 = w(γ)w(γ ⊥) = (1 + a)w(γ ⊥)
from which it follows w(γ ⊥) = 1
1+a = 1+ a+a2+· · ·+an−1 as we are in characteristic

3. Continuous Kakeya Line Configurations in connected Lie gro ups
Let G be a connected Lie group and g its Lie algebra. Recall that the Lie
subalgebras of g are in bijective correspondence with the connected subgroups of
G. (See [Wa]). The 1-dimensional subspaces of g are automatically Lie subalgebras
and are in bijective correspondence (exponential-logarithm corre spondence) with
the 1-parameter subgroups H of G. In the case of G = ( Rn, +), these are exactly
the lines through the origin. For any x ∈ G, xH is a left coset of H which will serve
as a ”parallel” copy of H going through the point x.
Thus in the setting of connected Lie groups, the left cosets of 1-p arameter sub-
groups will play the role that lines did in Rn. P (g), the projective space of the vector
space g, then serves as the space of ”parallel-classes” i.e., the space of 1- parameter
subgroups of G. For L ∈ P (g), we will denote the corresponding 1-parameter group
by exp(L). These constructions have many analogies with the Rn-case (and indeed
reduce to it when G = (Rn, +)):
(1) Through any point x in G, and L ∈ P (g), there is a unique left coset (”parallel
line”) x exp(L) which goes through x.
(2) ”Parallel lines” do not meet.
(3) For each L ∈ g, there is a smooth surjective homomorphism
(R, +) → exp(L) ⊆ G.
However the reader is warned that even though this map is an immers ion, the image
can be a circle group (map need not be injective) and the image does n ot have to
be closed in G (for example a dense line in a torus).
Similarly ﬁxing a positive deﬁnite inner product on g, the sphere S(g) functions
as the space of oriented one-parameter subgroups of G, where the orientation is
induced by moving outward from 0 to the point x ∈ S(g) in the corresponding
one-parameter subgroup of G.

Kakeya line conﬁguration in G is given by a continuous map σ : S(g) → G, where
S(g) is the unit-sphere in g.
A diﬃculty one ﬁrst encounters in a general Lie group is that the und erlying
space |σ | of a conﬁguration σ lives in G while the direction space P (g) lives in
the Lie algebra g. To help deal with this we make use of the exponential map
exp : g → G and make the following deﬁnition:
Deﬁnition 3.2. A continuous unoriented Kakeya line conﬁguration σ : P (g) → G
is called linear if there exists a continuous lift µ : P (g) → g such that exp ◦µ = σ .
We make a similar deﬁnition in the oriented case where S(g) replaces P (g).
The exponential map is a smooth map from g to G but it need not be surjective
in general even for connected Lie groups. (For example for the co nnected Lie group
G = SL(2, R) the exponential map is not surjective. ) Thus not every continuo us
Kakeya line conﬁguration in G will be linear, for example if the image of σ does
not lie in the image of the exponential map, there can be no linearizing lif t.
However as the derivative of the exponential map at 0 ∈ g is the identity map
g → g, the exponential induces a diﬀeomorphism from an open neighborho od of 0
in g to an open neighborhood of the identity element e in G. We make the following
deﬁnition:
Deﬁnition 3.3. A continuous unoriented Kakeya line conﬁguration σ : P (g) → G
is called taut if the image of σ lies in an open neighborhood of e in G which
is diﬀeomorphic to an open neighborhood of 0 in g via (the local inverse of ) the
exponential map. We make similar deﬁnitions in the oriented case. Note such
conﬁgurations are automatically linear.
Thus every connected Lie group has a lot of taut conﬁgurations th ough in general
the inclusions
Taut conﬁgurations ⊆ Linear conﬁgurations ⊆ Continuous conﬁgurations
are all proper. In ( Rn, +) these 3 notions coincide and we will see that in connected
nilpotent Lie groups of dimension > 2, that every continuous conﬁguration is linear.
First we prove analogs of our results in Rn with restrictions:
Theorem 3.4 (Linear Conﬁgurations) . Let G be a connected Lie group and let
σ : P (g) → G be a linear unoriented conﬁguration. Then e ∈ |σ |. A similar result
holds for linear oriented conﬁgurations as long as the dimen sion of G is odd.
Proof. Let µ : P (g) → g denote a continuous lift of σ . By the proof of Theorem 2.4,
we ﬁnd that there must exist a line L0 ∈ P (g) such that µ (L0) ∈ L0. Thus
σ (L0) ∈ exp(L0). This in turn implies exp( L0) ⊆ ∪ L∈P (g)(σ (L) ⋆ exp(L)) = |σ |. As
e ∈ exp(L0), we are done. The proof of the oriented case proceeds similarly us ing
the proof of Theorem 2.6 which imposes the restriction that the dime nsion of g and
hence of G is odd. □

4. Continuous Kakeya Line Configurations in homogeneous spac es
Let G be a connected Lie group and K a closed subgroup. Then it is well known
that the projection map π : G → K\G gives a ﬁber bundle with ﬁber K and that
the homogeneous space K\G is a smooth manifold. (See [Wa]). In fact, this ﬁber
bundle is a principal K-bundle. The translates of one-parameter subgroups in G
map to a distinguished class of curves in K\G. (These can be constant curve if the
one-parameter subgroup lies in K for example).
Deﬁnition 4.1. A continuous unoriented G-Kakeya conﬁguration in X = K\G is
a continuous map σ : P (g) → X. We deﬁne |σ | = ∪ L∈g(σ (L) ⋆ exp(L)) where ⋆
denotes the right action of G on X. We say the conﬁguration lifts to a conﬁguration
in G if there is a continuous unoriented Kakeya conﬁguration in G, ˆσ : P (g) → G
such that π ◦ ˆσ = σ where π : G → K\G is the quotient map. Notice in this case
that |σ | = π (|ˆσ |). We make similar deﬁnitions for oriented Kakeya conﬁgurati ons
where P (g) is replaced with S(g) for some choice of positive deﬁnite inner product
on g.
Notice that as ﬁber bundles are Serre ﬁbrations, the question of w hen a contin-
uous map like σ lifts is a homotopy question, i.e., if σ1 is homotopic to σ2 then
σ1 has a continuous lift if and only if σ2 does. Thus in asking when a G-Kakeya
conﬁguration σ in X lifts to one in G, only the unbased homotopy class of σ is
relevant.
The following lemma follows immediately from this observation:
Lemma 4.2. Let σ : P (g) → X = K\G be a continuous unoriented G-Kakeya
conﬁguration in X whose image set lies in a contractible subspace of X (like a
chart). Then σ is homotopic to a constant map and hence has a continuous lift to
a continuous unoriented Kakeya conﬁguration in G. Similar statements hold for
oriented conﬁgurations.
In general a continuous oriented G-Kakeya conﬁguration σ : S(g) → X = K\G
determines a unbased homotopy class in [ Sn−1, X ] where n is the dimension of G.
As X is path connected, this in turn determines a π1(X)-orbit C in πn−1(X) under
the action of π1(X) on π∗(X). The conﬁguration lifts to a continuous oriented
Kakeya conﬁguration in G if and only if some element in C is in the image of
π∗ : πn−1(G) → πn−1(X) if and only if some element in C is in the kernel of
the boundary operator ∂ : πn−1(X) → πn−2(K). Using these observations and
similar variants for projective space, the liftability question can be r esolved in most
examples.

Corollary 4.3. Let G be a connected solvable Lie group of type E and K a closed
subgroup with corresponding homogeneous space X = K\G. If σ is an unoriented
continuous G-Kakeya conﬁguration in X which lifts to one in G, then |σ | = X.

odd.
Let us look at one simple example to illustrate working with Kakeya conﬁ g-
urations in general homogeneous spaces. Let G = SO(3), then G is a compact
connected Lie group which is homeomorphic to RP 3 as a space. The Lie algebra
so(3) is isomorphic to the Lie algebra given by R3 with the cross-product as bracket.
Given a nonzero vector ˆv in the Lie algebra, the exponential ﬂow generates the ro-
tation about the axis given by ˆv in the counter clockwise direction (right hand rule).
Thus the one-parameter subgroup of SO(3) corresponding to any line in R3 is just
the set of rotations about that line.
Now consider the homogeneous space SO(2)\SO(3) which is diﬀeomorphic to the
standard sphere S2 ⊆ R3. Under the quotient map, the translates of one-parameter
subgroups map to closed geodesics (great circles or constant cur ves). A continuous
oriented SO(3)-Kakeya conﬁguration in S2 is then a map σ : S(R3) → S2 which
represents a continuously varying family of these curves in S2 which cover all the
”directions” in SO(3). (Thus there can be redundancy in the directions in S2).
(To avoid such redundancy in general, a choice of identiﬁcation of th e tangent
space of the homogeneous space with a subspace of the tangent s pace of the Lie
group G can be chosen and only the corresponding subspace of the projec tive space
of g used. However this requires a choice of horizontal lift in the ﬁber bu ndle, i.e.,
a choice of connection for the principal K-bundle. We will not pursue this topic
here.)
Since π2(SO(3)) = 0, an oriented SO(3)-Kakeya conﬁguration in S2, σ : S(R3) =
S2 → S2 lifts to an oriented Kakeya conﬁguration in SO(3) if and only if it has
degree 0 as a map, i.e., is homotopic to a constant.
